What this manuscript is about
In this work, we introduce PMPNN-DDG, where the recently proposed message passing encoder
decoder-based autoregressive protein design model, ProteinMPNN [1], is utilized for extracting
a minimal set of interpretable features, that can ideally quantify the structural frustration
induced by single point mutations. Subsequently, these features are combined with position-
specific scoring matrix (PSSM)-derived evolutionary conservation features, before training a
Random Forest (RF) regressor model, which outperforms the existing state-of-the-art DDG
prediction models described in [2], on several benchmark datasets.




2. Benchmark Datasets
It is to be noted that the three independent test sets were kept separate throughout the model
training and hyperparameter tuning stages, to ensure valid comparison with the existing
methods.




3. Description of MPNN-Extracted Features
As mentioned in the introduction section, a novel contribution of this work is the PMPNN-based
feature extraction method, which encodes each single point mutation using a length-nine real
vector. These nine features will be denoted by A, B, C, D, E-1 , E-2, E-3, E-4, and E-5 throughout the
rest of this manuscript (the last five features can be grouped together as these are generated by
the same function, thus E will be used to denote these five numbers as a set) . Before each of the
five feature generation functions are detailed in individual subsections, our general procedure to
identify most attended neighbors, mask the identified neighbors one by one, extract
corresponding embedding vectors, corresponding message vectors, and corresponding
probability distributions from PMPNN for quantifying the shift in structural stability induced by
wildtype-to-mutant residue-identity change, is explained in the following subsection (the phrases
“most attended neighbors”, “mask neighbors”, “embedding vectors”, and “message vectors” are
described as well).

3.1. Neighbor Ranking and Neighbor-Vector Extraction Procedure
We hypothesize that, the changes in the center->neighbor message vectors, and the changes in
the neighbor embedding vectors due to the change in embedding of the center node reflect the
structural stability shift due to a particular single point mutation under consideration (since this
specific mutation information provided to PMPNN has caused the message and embedding
vectors to change in the first place with an attempt to alleviate potential structural frustration).
Consequently, we establish a protocol to systematically extract these vectors for both the
wildtype and the mutated sequences, which is the basis of our features B, C, D, and E. This
protocol is illustrated in Figure 2.




   Figure 2: Our proposed procedure for extracting features from PMPNN is depicted in this
                                          diagram.



As depicted in Figure 2, the first step is to pass the wildtype protein through PMPNN, where the
wildtype backbone is processed through the encoder layers before wildtype sequence
embeddings are integrated at the decoder layers. At this step, the mutation position is masked
in the sequence input, thus PMPNN attempts to reconstruct that position based on full backbone
geometry and residue identity of all other positions, which are unmasked. The mutation position
will be referred to as the “center”/ “center node” throughout the rest of this section, since PMNN
treats the input backbone as a graph, where every residue is connected to 48 spatially least
distant residues (residues with lowest pairwise Cα-Cα distances) through directed edges, and the
48 residues closest to the mutation position will be referred to as “neighbors”/ “neighbor nodes”.
Next, for each of these 48 neighbors, we extract a 128-length vector that the center node receives
from the respective neighbor at the last decoder layer to update its own embedding (“neighbor-
>center message vector”). Consequently, each of these vectors can be regarded as a specific
neighbor’s contribution to the center node’s identity, thus we utilize the L2-norms of these
vectors for ranking the neighbors based on their interaction strength with mutated position in
the wildtype structure (higher L2-norm would indicate stronger interaction) and select K
neighbors with the highest norms (K was set to 15 based on empirical observations). These
neighbors, referred to as “Top K Neighbors” in Figure 2, can also be regarded as “the most
attended neighbors” by the center node, since some hypothetical attention module’s coefficients
would be used as scaling factors for the message vectors before summation [3], and we are
approximating those coefficients by taking norms of those vectors since PMPNN does not use
any explicit attention module. The positions of these top-K neighbors are saved at this point and
utilized in the next step of our pipeline. Additionally, feature A is generated from the probability
distribution corresponding to the masked center node extracted during this step of our pipeline
(this point is discussed further in one of the following subsections, and the corresponding output
vector is denoted by PC in Figure 2).

In the next step, each of the top-K neighbor positions are masked one by one separately, while
the mutation position is unmasked, set to both the wildtype residue and the mutant residue,
which results in two sets of vectors (embedding vector, center->neighbor message vector, and
probability distribution vector) for each neighbor position, one set corresponding to the wildtype
center, and another set corresponding to the mutant center. The embedding vector, center-
>neighbor message vector, and probability distribution corresponding to the wildtype residue for
neighbor j are denoted by EjWT, MjWT, and PjWT, respectively, whereas the vectors in the same order
corresponding to the mutated residue for neighbor j are denoted by EjMT, MjMT, and PjMT,
respectively. In the described setting, since the differences between the equivalent vectors for
the same neighbor position (i.e., EjWT vs. EjMT, MjWT vs. MjMT, and PjWT vs. PjMT) are conditioned on
the change in center embedding due to WT->MT mutation, we utilize these differences for
characterizing mutation impact on the most attended neighbors, and encode these vectors
through the features B, C, D, and E, described in the following subsection.

3.4. Feature-Label Correlation and Feature-Feature Decorrelation Analysis
While extracting the features A, B, C, D, and E from PMPNN one by one, we attempt to get an
initial estimation of how potent these individually are for the task of experimental DDG
prediction, and how they might perform together when combined through a downstream ML
model. For this purpose, we examine the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) of each of the
features with experimental DDG labels along with the pairwise feature-feature PCC values on
S_2648 training set, which are reported through the correlation heatmap in Figure 4.




             Figure 4: PMPPN extracted feature-feature Correlation-Heatmap for
              S_2648 training instances. DDG refers to experimental DDG labels.


As can be seen from Figure 4, among A, B, C, and D, all pairs seem “sufficiently” decorrelated
(e.g., the maximum pairwise PCC of 0.52 is observed between A and B). On the other hand,
feature C shows a PCC of -0.15 with the corresponding experimental DDG values, which is the
minimum feature-label correlation in terms of PCC magnitude.

Consequently, some interesting patterns emerge after PCC values corresponding to E-1, E-2, E-3,
E-4, and E-5 are included in the heatmap, which provide insight into the information captured by
the PCA components, and their potential contribution to downstream ML model. E-1 shows a
PCC of 0.49 with D, indicating that the maximum variation in center->neighbor message
difference vectors could be largely explained by the changes in corresponding neighbor
embedding vectors (this is somewhat intuitive since some amount of neighbor embedding
change can be attributed to last decoder layer center->neighbor message change). On the other
hand, E-2, which has the highest PCC magnitude with experimental labels among the five PCA-
based features, is not “significantly” correlated with any other feature (maximum PCC of 0.17
with D), thus making it highly probable that, incorporation of E-2 will boost downstream ML
model’s performance. Similarly, E-4 has a PCC of 0.21 with the labels but has a maximum PCC of
0.20 with other features, which makes it another great candidate for incorporation into the
combined feature set. While E-5 has low PCC with both the label column and the other features
(this component could probably be removed without hurting model performance), E-3 has PCC
values of 0.55, 0.40, and 0.55 with A, B, and C, respectively, and PCC of 0.26 with the label column
(features A, B, and C could probably account for this component if it were to be removed from
the final feature set). Furthermore, E-1, E-2, E-3, E-4, and E-5 seem “sufficiently” decorrelated
among themselves (e.g., the maximum pairwise PCC of 0.20 is observed between E-2 and E-4).

Based on the above discussion, it can be stated that, our proposed attributes A, B, C, and D
contain significant discriminatory information with inherent biophysical interpretation, while
group E increases information content of the combined set by supplementing the feature space
with dimensions where abstract aspects of mutation structural impact are captured.

4. Description of Evolutionary Features (F, G, and H)
Almost all the top performing sequence-based and/or structure-based DDG prediction tools (e.g.,
PremPS [6], ACDC-NN [7], DDGun3D [8], INPS-Seq [9], etc.) compared in [2], have explicitly
incorporated sequence-level mutation-induced evolutionary conservation disruption
information extracted through PSI-BLAST [10], or HMMER3 [11], or PROVEAN [12]; either as
manually extracted features, or as part of sequence embedding provided to NN-based feature
extraction modules. These features have usually been ranked as the top contributing features
[6], thus indicating that the downstream ML models or NN architectures are at some level
learning to extract structural stability related signals from mutation-position and/or mutation-
neighborhood evolutionary patterns, and map those signals to DDG labels. Since our PMPNN
features A, B, C, D, and E, do not contain any explicit multiple sequence alignment (MSA)- derived
information, we explore the possibility of embellishing our final feature space by adding three
MSA-derived evolutionary conservation features.

Our evolutionary features were extracted from position-specific-scoring matrices (PSSM)
characterizing protein-specific homologous sequences retrieved by running PSI-BLAST program
[10] on UniRef90 database [13], using default parameters described in [6]. For a particular
mutation, these three features are extracted from the row corresponding to the PDB sequence
position at which the mutation has occurred and the two columns of that row containing the log-
odds ratios corresponding to the occurrences of the wildtype residue and the alternate residue
at the mutation site. These three evolutionary features are referred to as F, G, and H, throughout
the rest of this manuscript.

Finally, we perform pairwise decorrelation analysis on the full feature-set (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, and
H) to get an estimation of how the evolutionary features (F, G, and H) might embellish the already
informative feature space spanned by our PMPNN extracted features (A, B, C, D, and E), which is
shown in Figure 5.
          Figure 5: Final feature-set all-pair Correlation-Heatmap for S_2648 training
          instances. DDG refers to experimental DDG labels. A, B, C, D, and E, are our
            proposed PMPNN extracted features, while F, G, and H are widely used

As can be seen from Figure 5, features F, G, and H show maximum PCC magnitudes of 0.32, 0.15,
and 0.39 (E-1) with A, A, and E-1, respectively, thus indicating that most of the dimensions in our
final feature space (the space spanned by PMPNN extracted features + evolutionary features)
contain significantly non-overlapping information encoding different aspects of the mutation
under consideration. Having said that, it is to be re-iterated that, E-3 might be removed without
decreasing performance of the downstream ML model since the variation along E-3 is explained
by A, B, and C (some linear combination of these three features might recapitulate E-3 with
substantially high accuracy), and E-1 might also be removed due to its PCC magnitudes with both
D and F. However, for this work, we choose to retain all five of the kernel-PCA projection features,
and plan to address this issue with an attempt to improve downstream ML model performance
further as part of future work.

5. Regression Model
The primary focus of our current work is to put together a workflow that can extract a small non-
redundant set of informative and interpretable features from PMPNN, combine those with
discriminative and “sufficiently” decorrelated evolutionary features, and establish the efficacy of
the final feature set in predicting experimental DDG values by comparing with state-of-the-art
methods presented in [2], rather than achieving the maximum possible performance on the
independent sets. Therefore, we have not put much effort into optimal model-type selection and
hyperparameter tuning. We have employed Random Forest (RF) [14] regression implemented in
the scikit-learn package [15] for mapping the feature vectors to experimental DDG values.

We performed an extremely coarse-grained manual tuning for “n_estimators” and
“max_samples” hyperparameters on some validation splits composed of all mutations from 25
randomly selected proteins from S_2648 training set while training on mutations from rest of the
S_2648 proteins. We selected “n_estimators” to be 500 and “max_samples” was set to 0.5, while
default scikit-learn values for all other hyperparameters were retained. We acknowledge that,
our predictor’s performance could improve further if a grid search-based extensive
hyperparameter tuning was performed on more methodically held out validation partitions,
however, those empirical analyses are out of the scope of our current work.

7. Results and Discussion

7.1. Feature Contributions:
As can be observed from Figure 6 for the independents test sets S_669 and Ssym, respectively,
adding features A, B, C, D, E, F, G, and H one by one during training the RF model on S_2648
training instances does not result in performance decrease between any two consecutive feature
additions, thus indicating that, our final feature set does not result in excessive dimensionality-
induced overfitting. Moreover, up to the first evolutionary feature F, each dimension addition
results in performance improvement for both independent sets, thus corroborating our
decorrelation analysis-derived hypothesis regarding non-overlapping information content of the
dimensions in the final space. However, addition of the last two evolutionary features G and H
does not seem to result in further improvement (more pronounced for S_669), which is again in
agreement with pairwise correlation values (G and H have PCC values of 0.78 and -0.79 with F,
respectively). Nevertheless, we retain features G and H for now as that does not interfere with
the primary focus of this work. It is to be noted that, for each of the results reported in Figure 6,
RF was trained ten times on S_2648, and PCC values derived from test set predictions of those
ten models were averaged.

7.2. Performance on Forward and Reverse Mutations, and Anti-
Symmetry of Predictions:
Next, we investigate the performance of the RF model trained on our full feature set
(A+B+C+D+E+F+G+H) on forward and reverse mutations of the test sets separately and report
the correlation between predictions for forward and reverse pairs. Reverse mutations for S_2648
instances were added during training as done in recent DDG prediction methods [2] for
incentivizing the RF model to perform well on both stabilizing and de-stabilizing mutations. PCC
of -1 between forward and reverse predictions on the test sets would indicate that our predictor
is perfectly antisymmetric, which is a highly desirable yet difficult to incorporate property for
DDG predictors, since free energy change introduced by a forward mutation plus the change
induced by its reverse mutation should be equal to zero [9]. Additionally, since majority of the
forward mutations are in the first quadrant and most of the reverse mutations are in the third
quadrant, correlation between predictions and experimental DDGs calculated on
(forward+reverse) mutations might result in higher correlation compared to correlations on only
forward and only reverse mutations, thus making it important to check only forward, only
reverse, (forward+reverse), and (forward vs. reverse) correlations for comprehensive evaluation
of a DDG predictor. Only forward correlation, only reverse correlation, (forward+reverse)
correlation, (forward vs. reverse) correlation, only forward root mean square error (RMSE), only
reverse root mean square error, and (forward+reverse) root mean square error, denoted by r F,
rR, rF+R, rF-R, rmsF, rmsR, and rmsF+R, respectively, are reported in Table 1.

As can be seen from Table 1, as mentioned in [6], and observed for every DDG antisymmetric
DDG predictor in [2], rF+r is significantly higher than both rF and rR on all three independent test
sets. Moreover, PCC and RMSE on forward and reverse mutations are equal, which indicate that
PMNPP-DDG performs equally well for stabilizing and destabilizing mutations, and rF-R of -0.99
points towards the impressive antisymmetric property of predicted DDG values.
