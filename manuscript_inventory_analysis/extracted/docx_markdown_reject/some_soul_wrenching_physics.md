One of the many implications of Einstein\'s special relativity work is
that time moves relative to the observer. An object in motion
experiences time dilation, meaning that when an object is moving very
fast it experiences time more slowly than when it is at rest.

For example, when astronaut Scott Kelly spent nearly a year aboard
the [International Space
Station](https://www.space.com/16748-international-space-station.html) starting
in 2015, he was moving much faster than his twin brother, astronaut Mark
Kelly, who spent the year on the planet\'s surface. Due to time
dilation, Mark Kelly [aged just a little faster than
Scott](https://www.space.com/33411-astronaut-scott-kelly-relativity-twin-brother-ages.html) ---
\"five milliseconds,\" according to the earth-bound twin. Since Scott
wasn\'t moving near lightspeed, the actual difference in aging due to
time dilation was negligible. In fact, considering how much stress and
radiation the airborne twin experienced aboard the ISS, some would argue
Scott Kelly increased his rate of aging.

But at speeds approaching the speed of light, the effects of time
dilation could be much more apparent. Imagine a 15-year-old leaves her
high school traveling at 99.5% of the speed of light for five years
(from the teenage astronaut\'s perspective). When the 15-year-old got
back to Earth, she would have aged those 5 years she spent traveling.
Her classmates, however, would be 65 years old --- 50 years would have
passed on the much slower-moving planet.

We don\'t currently have the technology to travel anywhere near that
speed. But with the precision of modern technology, time dilation does
actually affect human engineering.

GPS devices work by calculating a position based on communication with
at least three satellites in distant Earth orbits. Those satellites have
to keep track of incredibly precise time in order to pinpoint a location
on the planet, so they work based on atomic clocks. But because those
atomic clocks are on board satellites that are constantly whizzing
through space at 8,700 mph (14,000 km/h), special relativity means that
they tick an extra 7 microseconds, or 7 millionths of a second, each
day, according to American Physical Society publication [Physics
Central](https://physicscentral.com/explore/writers/will.cfm). In order
to maintain pace with Earth clocks, atomic clocks on GPS satellites need
to subtract 7 microseconds each day.

With additional effects from general relativity (Einstein\'s follow-up
to special relativity that incorporates gravity), clocks closer to the
center of a large gravitational mass like Earth tick more slowly than
those farther away. That effect adds microseconds to each day on a GPS
atomic clock, so in the end engineers subtract 7 microseconds and add 45
more back on. GPS clocks don\'t tick over to the next day until they
have run a total of 38 microseconds longer than comparable clocks on
Earth.

**Law of Motion:**

The forces acting on a body [add as
vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector#Addition_and_subtraction),
and so the total force on a body depends upon both the magnitudes and
the directions of the individual forces. When the net force on a body is
equal to zero, then by Newton\'s second law, the body does not
accelerate, and it is said to be in [mechanical
equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_equilibrium). A
state of mechanical equilibrium is *stable* if, when the position of the
body is changed slightly, the body remains near that equilibrium.
Otherwise, the equilibrium is *unstable.*

Newton\'s second law is sometimes presented as a *definition* of force,
i.e., a force is that which exists when an inertial observer sees a body
accelerating. In order for this to be more than
a [tautology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tautology_(logic)) ---
acceleration implies force, force implies acceleration --- some other
statement about force must also be made. For example, an equation
detailing the force might be specified, like [Newton\'s law of universal
gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_law_of_universal_gravitation).
By inserting such an expression for {\\displaystyle {\\vec {F}}} into
Newton\'s second law, an equation with predictive power can be
written.[^\[note\ 6\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion#cite_note-26) Newton\'s
second law has also been regarded as setting out a research program for
physics, establishing that important goals of the subject are to
identify the forces present in nature and to catalogue the constituents
of matter.

Inertial frame of reference

An inertial frame of reference can be defined
in [analytical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_mechanics) terms
as a frame of reference that describes time and
space [homogeneously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneity_(physics)), isotropically,
and in a time-independent
manner.[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame_of_reference#cite_note-LandauMechanics-3) Conceptually,
the physics of a system in an inertial frame have no causes external to
the
system.[^\[4\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame_of_reference#cite_note-Ferraro-4) An
inertial frame of reference may also be called an **inertial reference
frame**, **inertial frame**, **Galilean reference frame**, or **inertial
space**.[^\[5\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame_of_reference#cite_note-5)

It is important to note that all inertial frames are in a state of
constant, [rectilinear](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/rectilinear) motion
with respect to one another; in other words,
an [accelerometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerometer) moving
with any of them would detect zero acceleration. Measurements in one
inertial frame can be converted to measurements in another by a simple
transformation (the [Galilean
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_transformation) in
Newtonian physics and the [Lorentz
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation) in

relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity), in any
region small enough for the curvature of spacetime and [tidal
forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_forces)[^\[6\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame_of_reference#cite_note-6) to
be negligible, one can find a set of inertial frames that approximately
describe that
region.^[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame_of_reference#cite_note-Einstein0-7)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame_of_reference#cite_note-Giulini-8)^

**Roche limit:**

In [celestial
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_mechanics),
the **Roche limit**, also called **Roche radius**, is the distance from
a celestial body within which a second celestial body, held together
only by its own force
of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity), will disintegrate
because the first body\'s [tidal
forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_force) exceed the second
body\'s gravitational
self-attraction.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roche_limit#cite_note-wolf-1) Inside
the Roche
limit, [orbiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_orbit) material
disperses and
forms [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_system_(astronomy)),
whereas outside the limit, material tends
to [coalesce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalescence_(physics)). The
Roche radius depends on the radius of the first body and on the ratio of
the bodies\' densities.

**Gravity creates stars and planets:**

**Gravity is what holds the planets in orbit around the sun** and what
keeps the moon in orbit around Earth. The gravitational pull of the moon
pulls the seas towards it, causing the ocean tides. Gravity creates
stars and planets by pulling together the material from which they are
made.

**Tidal Force**

The **tidal force** is a gravitational effect that stretches a body
along the line towards the [center of
mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_mass) of another body due
to a [gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient) (difference in
strength) in [gravitational
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) from the other
body; it is responsible for diverse phenomena,
including [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tide), [tidal
locking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tidal_locking), breaking apart of
celestial bodies and formation of [ring
systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_system) within the [Roche
limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roche_limit), and in extreme
cases, [spaghettification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaghettification) of
objects. It arises because the gravitational field exerted on one body
by another is not constant across its parts: the nearest side is
attracted more strongly than the farthest side. It is this difference
that causes a body to get stretched. Thus, the tidal force is also known
as the differential force, as well as a secondary effect of the
gravitational field.

**Gravitational Field:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), a **gravitational
field** is
a [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_model) used to
explain the influences that a massive body extends into the space around
itself, producing a force on another massive
body.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field#cite_note-1) Thus,
a
gravitational [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)) is
used to
explain [gravitational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) phenomena,
and is measured
in [newtons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_(unit)) per [kilogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram) (N/kg).
Equivalently, it is measured
in [meters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter) per [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second) squared
(m/s^2^).

In its original
concept, [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) was
a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) between
point [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass). Following [Isaac
Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton), [Pierre-Simon
Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre-Simon_Laplace) attempted
to model gravity as some kind
of [radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation) field
or [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid), and since the 19th
century, explanations for gravity have usually been taught in terms of a
field model, rather than a point attraction.

In a field model, rather than two particles attracting each other, the
particles
distort [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) via their
mass, and this distortion is what is perceived and measured as a
\"force\".^\[[*citation\ needed*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)\]^ In
such a model one states that matter moves in certain ways in response to
the curvature of
spacetime,[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field#cite_note-2) and
that there is either *no gravitational
force*,[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field#cite_note-3) or
that gravity is a [fictitious
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictitious_force).[^\[4\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field#cite_note-4)

Gravity is distinguished from other forces by its obedience to
the [equivalence
principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_principle).

**Spring Scale:**

A **spring scale**, **spring balance** or **newton meter** is a type of
mechanical [force
gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_gauge) or [weighing
scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighing_scale). It consists of
a [spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_(device)) fixed at one
end with a hook to attach an object at the other. It works in accordance
with [Hooke\'s Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke%27s_Law), which
states that the force needed to extend or compress a spring by some
distance scales linearly with respect to that distance. Therefore, the
scale markings on the spring balance are equally spaced.

A **spring balance** can be calibrated for the accurate measurement of
mass in the location in which they are used, but many spring balances
are marked right on their face \"Not Legal for Trade\" or words of
similar import due to the approximate nature of the theory used to mark
the scale. Also, the spring in the scale can permanently stretch with
repeated use.

A spring scale will only read correctly in a frame of reference where
the acceleration in the spring axis is constant (such as on earth, where
the acceleration is due to gravity). This can be shown by taking a
spring scale into an [elevator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator),
where the weight measured will change as the elevator moves up and down
changing velocities.

**Hooke\'s law:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), **Hooke\'s law** is
an [empirical law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical_law) which
states that the [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) (*F*)
needed to extend or compress
a [spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_(device)) by some
distance (*x*) [scales
linearly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_(mathematics)#Direct_proportionality) with
respect to that distance---that is, *F~s~* = *kx*, where *k* is a
constant factor characteristic of the spring (i.e.,
its [stiffness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stiffness)), and *x* is
small compared to the total possible deformation of the spring. The law
is named after 17th-century British physicist [Robert
Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke). He first stated the
law in 1676 as a
Latin [anagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagram).^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke%27s_law#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke%27s_law#cite_note-2)^ He
published the solution of his anagram in
1678[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke%27s_law#cite_note-3) as: *ut
tensio, sic vis* (\"as the extension, so the force\" or \"the extension
is proportional to the force\"). Hooke states in the 1678 work that he
was aware of the law since 1660.

Hooke\'s equation holds (to some extent) in many other situations where
an [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_(physics)) body
is [deformed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_(physics)), such
as wind blowing on a tall building, and a musician plucking
a [string](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_(music)) of a guitar. An
elastic body or material for which this equation can be assumed is said
to
be [linear-elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_elasticity) or **Hookean**.

Hooke\'s law is only a [first-order linear
approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series) to the real
response of springs and other elastic bodies to applied forces. It must
eventually fail once the forces exceed some limit, since no material can
be compressed beyond a certain minimum size, or stretched beyond a
maximum size, without some permanent deformation or change of state.
Many materials will noticeably deviate from Hooke\'s law well before
those [elastic limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_limit) are
reached.

**Spacetime Diagram:**

A **spacetime diagram** is a graphical illustration of the properties of
space and time in the [special theory of
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_theory_of_relativity).
Spacetime diagrams allow a qualitative understanding of the
corresponding phenomena like [time
dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_dilation) and [length
contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_contraction) without
mathematical equations.

The history of an object\'s location throughout all time traces out a
line, referred to as the object\'s [world
line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_line), in a spacetime diagram.
Points in spacetime diagrams represent a fixed position in space and
time and are referred to
as [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_(relativity)).

The most well-known class of spacetime diagrams are known as **Minkowski
diagrams**, developed by [Hermann
Minkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Minkowski) in 1908.
Minkowski diagrams are two-dimensional graphs that depict events as
happening in
a [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) consisting of one
space dimension and one time dimension. Unlike a regular distance-time
graph, the distance is displayed on the horizontal axis and time on the
vertical axis. Additionally, the time and space [units of
measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement) are
chosen in such a way that an object moving at the speed of light is
depicted as following a 45° angle to the diagram\'s axes.

Kinematics and kinetics are sub-areas of biomechanics. Kinematics is the
study of ***description*** of motion while kinetics is the study
of ***explanation*** of motion. In kinematics, the focus is on
the ***motion*** of the object, while kinetics focuses on
the ***cause*** of motion dealing with the \'why.\'

For example, in the study of golf swing, kinematics focuses on details
of the swing \'motion\' such as the shape of the clubhead path,
positions of the body and club at various swing events, velocities of
the body parts and club, and the timing of slow-down of the body for
speed-up of the club. In order to describe the swing motion objectively,
it is important to accurately measure the motion first. This is why
measurement of the motion is one of the central aspects of kinematics.
Basic kinematic quantities include time, position, displacement
(distance), velocity (speed), and acceleration. In addition to these,
shapes of trajectories of various points on the body, club and
orientation of motion planes of various body segments and club are also
kinematic issues. A complex motion of an object can be resolved into the
linear motion of the center of mass (COM) of the body and the angular
motion of the body about its COM, which is also a kinematic issue. (See
the [Linear Motion vs. Angular
Motion](http://drkwongolf.info/biom/lin-ang.html) page for the
definitions of linear and angular motion.) The [kinematic
sequence](http://drkwongolf.info/biom/kinem-seq.html) plot is based on
the angular velocity patterns of body segments, lines, and club.

• Kinematics: The term kinematics means motion. Kinematics is the study

• Dynamics: On the other hand, dynamics is the study of the causes of

The **invariant mass**, **rest mass**, **intrinsic mass**, **proper
mass**, or in the case of bound systems simply **mass**, is the portion
of the total mass of
an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_body) or [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) of
objects that is independent of the overall motion of the system. More
precisely, it is a characteristic of the system\'s
total [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum) that
is the same in all [frames of
reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frames_of_reference) related
by [Lorentz
transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_mass#cite_note-LawrenceS-1) If
a [center-of-momentum
frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center-of-momentum_frame) exists
for the system, then the invariant mass of a system is equal to its
total mass in that \"rest frame\". In other reference frames, where the
system\'s momentum is nonzero, the total mass (a.k.a. [relativistic
mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_mass)) of the system is
greater than the invariant mass, but the invariant mass remains
unchanged.

Because of [mass--energy
equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence),
the **rest energy** of the system is simply the invariant mass times
the [speed of
light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) squared. Similarly,
the total energy of the system is its total (relativistic) mass times
the speed of light squared.

The word [*mass*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) has two meanings
in [special
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity): [*invariant
mass*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_mass) (also called rest
mass) is an invariant quantity which is the same for all observers in
all reference frames, while the *relativistic mass* is dependent on the
velocity of the observer. According to the concept of [mass--energy
equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence),
invariant mass is equivalent to *rest energy*, while relativistic mass
is equivalent to *relativistic energy* (also called total energy).

The term \"relativistic mass\" tends not to be used in particle and
nuclear physics and is often avoided by writers on special relativity,
in favor of referring to the body\'s relativistic
energy.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_in_special_relativity#cite_note-roche-1) In
contrast, \"invariant mass\" is usually preferred over rest energy. The
measurable inertia and the warping of spacetime by a body in a given
frame of reference is determined by its relativistic mass, not merely
its invariant mass. For example, photons have zero rest mass but
contribute to the inertia (and weight in a gravitational field) of any
system containing them.

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), **mass--energy
equivalence** is the relationship
between [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) and [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) in
a system\'s [rest frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rest_frame),
where the two values differ only by a constant and the units of
measurement.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence#cite_note-Serway1217-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence#cite_note-G%C3%BCnther-2)^ The
principle is described by the physicist [Albert
Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein)\'s famous
formula: [{\\displaystyle
E=mc\^{2}}](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Special:MathWikibase&qid=Q35875).[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass%E2%80%93energy_equivalence#cite_note-famous-3)

**rest frame:**

In [special
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity),
the **rest frame** of
a [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle) is the [frame of
reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) (a [coordinate
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_system) attached to
physical markers) in which the particle is at rest.

The rest frame of compound objects (such as a fluid, or a solid made of
many vibrating [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom)) is taken to
be the frame of reference in which the
average [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum) of the
particles which make up the substance is zero (the particles may
individually have momentum, but collectively have no net momentum). The
rest frame of a container of gas, for example, would be the rest frame
of the container itself, in which the gas molecules are not at rest, but
are no more likely to be traveling in one direction than another. The
rest frame of a river would be the frame of an unpowered boat, in which
the mean velocity of the water is zero. This frame is also called the
center-of-mass frame, or [center-of-momentum
frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center-of-momentum_frame).

**Linear Motion vs. Angular Motion**

Any complex motion of a body can be broken down to two simple motions:
linear motion and angular motion. ***Linear motion*** is translation
from one position to another while ***angular motion*** is rotation
about an axis or center of rotation. Linear motion can also be viewed as
motion of a point and have two types: rectilinear (straight path) and
curvilinear (curved path).

This club motion can then be broken down to two simple motions: linear
motion of the mid-hand point (Figure 2A) and angular motion of the club
about the mid-hand (Figure 2B). In this case, the mid-hand point was
selected as the representative point of the club as the focus is how the
hands guide the club and how the club rotates around the hands. This is
actually a common practice in biomechanics to break down a complex
motion to linear motion of a point on the object and angular motion of
the object about the point. It is easier to handle these simple motions
separately than the actual complex motion at once. Actually, since
linear motion and angular motion are very different in nature, these
must be separated for an in-depth analysis.

**Momentum:**

In [Newtonian

product of

possessing a magnitude and a direction. If *m* is an object\'s mass

**Frame of Reference:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy),
a **frame of reference** (or **reference frame**) is an
abstract [coordinate
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_system) whose [origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_(mathematics)), [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_(geometry)),
and [scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_(geometry)) are
specified by a set of **reference points**―[geometric
points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_(geometry)) whose [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_(geometry)) is
identified both mathematically (with numerical coordinate values) and
physically (signaled by conventional markers)

For *n* dimensions, *n* + 1 reference points are sufficient to fully
define a reference frame. Using [rectangular Cartesian
coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate_system),
a reference frame may be defined with a reference point at the origin
and a reference point at one unit distance along each of
the *n* coordinate [axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_(mathematics)).^\[[*citation\ needed*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)\]^

In [Einsteinian
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity),

moving [observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer_(special_relativity)) and
the phenomenon under observation. In this context, the term often
becomes ***observational frame of reference*** or ***observational
reference frame***, which implies that the observer is at rest in the
frame, although not necessarily located at
its [origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origin_(mathematics)). A
relativistic reference frame includes (or implies) the [coordinate
time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_time), which does not
equate across different reference frames [moving
relatively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_motion) to each
other. The situation thus differs from [Galilean
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_invariance), in which
all possible coordinate times are essentially
equivalent.^\[[*citation\ needed*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed)\]^

**Scaling:**

In [affine
geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_geometry), **uniform
scaling** (or **[isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) scaling**[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_(geometry)#cite_note-1))
is a [linear
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) that
enlarges (increases) or shrinks (diminishes) objects by a [*scale
factor*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_factor) that is the same in
all directions. The result of uniform scaling
is [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_(geometry)) (in
the geometric sense) to the original. A scale factor of 1 is normally
allowed, so
that [congruent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_(geometry)) shapes
are also classed as similar. Uniform scaling happens, for example, when
enlarging or reducing
a [photograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photograph), or when
creating a [scale model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_model) of a
building, car, airplane, etc.

More general is **scaling** with a separate scale factor for each axis
direction. **Non-uniform
scaling** (**[anisotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anisotropic) scaling**)
is obtained when at least one of the scaling factors is different from
the others; a special case is **directional
scaling** or **stretching** (in one direction). Non-uniform scaling
changes the [shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape) of the object;
e.g. a square may change into a rectangle

# Time dilation

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity), **time
dilation** is the difference in the
elapsed [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_in_physics) as
measured by two clocks. It is either due to a
relative [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) between them
([special
relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity) \"kinetic\"
time dilation) or to a difference in [gravitational
potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential) between
their locations ([general
relativistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity) [gravitational
time
dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_time_dilation)).
When unspecified, \"time dilation\" usually refers to the effect due to
velocity.

# Gravitational time dilation

**Gravitational time dilation** is a form of [time
dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_dilation), an actual
difference of elapsed time between
two [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_(relativity)) as
measured
by [observers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observer_(special_relativity)) situated
at varying distances from a
gravitating [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass). The lower
the [gravitational
potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential) (the
closer the clock is to the source of gravitation), the slower time
passes, speeding up as the gravitational potential increases (the clock
getting away from the source of gravitation). [Albert
Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) originally
predicted this effect in his [theory of
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity) and it
has since been confirmed by [tests of general
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tests_of_general_relativity).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_time_dilation#cite_note-1)

This has been demonstrated by noting that [atomic
clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_clock) at differing
altitudes (and thus different gravitational potential) will eventually
show different times. The effects detected in such Earth-bound
experiments are extremely small, with differences being measured
in [nanoseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanosecond). Relative to
Earth\'s age in billions of years, Earth\'s core is effectively 2.5
years younger than its
surface.[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_time_dilation#cite_note-2) Demonstrating
larger effects would require greater distances from the Earth or a
larger gravitational source.

# World line

The **world line** (or **worldline**) of an object is
the [path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_(topology)) that an object
traces in
4-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension) [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime).
It is an important concept in
modern [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), and
particularly [theoretical
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics).

The concept of a \"world line\" is distinguished from concepts such as
an \"[orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit)\" or a
\"[trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory)\" (e.g., a
planet\'s *orbit in space* or the *trajectory* of a car on a road) by
the *time* dimension,

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), a world line of an
object (approximated as a point in space, e.g., a particle or observer)
is the sequence
of [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) events
corresponding to the history of the object. A world line is a special
type of curve in spacetime. Below an equivalent definition will be
explained: A world line is a time-like curve in spacetime. Each point of
a world line is an event that can be labeled with the time and the
spatial position of the object at that time.

Two world lines that start out separately and then intersect, signify
a *collision* or \"encounter\". Two world lines starting at the same
event in spacetime, each following its own path afterwards, may
represent the decay of a particle into two others or the emission of one
particle by another.

# Proper time

In [relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity), **proper
time** (from Latin, meaning *own time*) along a timelike [world
line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_line) is defined as
the [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) as measured by
a [clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock) following that line. It
is thus independent of coordinates, and is a [Lorentz
scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_scalar).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_time#cite_note-1) The **proper
time interval** between
two [events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_(relativity)) on a
world line is the change in proper time. This interval is the quantity
of interest, since proper time itself is fixed only up to an arbitrary
additive constant, namely the setting of the clock at some event along
the world line.

**Lorentz scalar:**

In a [relativistic
theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_relativity) of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics),
a **Lorentz scalar** is an expression, formed from items of the theory,
which evaluates to
a [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_(physics)), [invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_(physics)) under
any [Lorentz
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation). A
Lorentz scalar may be generated from e.g., the scalar product of
vectors, or from contracting tensors of the theory. While the components
of vectors and tensors are in general altered under Lorentz
transformations, Lorentz scalars remain unchanged.

**Lorentz transformations:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), the **Lorentz
transformations** are a six-parameter family
of [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) [transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_transformation) from
a [coordinate
frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_frame) in [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) to
another frame that moves at a constant velocity relative to the former.
The respective inverse transformation is then parameterized by the
negative of this velocity. The transformations are named after the
Dutch [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) [Hendrik
Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz).

**Point:**

In classical [Euclidean
geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry),
a **point** is a [primitive
notion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_notion) that models an
exact location in
the [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space), and has no
length, width, or
thickness.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_(geometry)#cite_note-1) In
modern [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics),
a **point** refers more generally to
an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_(mathematics)) of
some [set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics)) called
a [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_(mathematics)).

Being a primitive notion means that a point cannot be defined in terms
of previously defined objects. That is, a point is defined only by some
properties, called [axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom), that
it must satisfy; for example, *\"there is exactly
one [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(geometry)) that passes
through two different points\"*.

**Lorentz transformations:**

In [[physics]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics),
the **Lorentz transformations** are a six-parameter family
of [[linear]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) [[transformations]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_transformation) from
a [[coordinate
frame]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_frame) in [[spacetime]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime) to
another frame that moves at a constant velocity relative to the former.
The respective inverse transformation is then parameterized by the
negative of this velocity. The transformations are named after the
Dutch [[physicist]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) [[Hendrik
Lorentz]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz).

The most common form of the transformation, parametrized by the real
constant {\\displaystyle v,} representing a velocity confined to
the *x*-direction, is expressed
as^[[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformation#cite_note-2)]{.underline}^

{\\displaystyle {\\begin{aligned}t\'&=\\gamma \\left(t-{\\frac
{vx}{c\^{2}}}\\right)\\\\x\'&=\\gamma
\\left(x-vt\\right)\\\\y\'&=y\\\\z\'&=z\\end{aligned}}}

where (*t*, *x*, *y*, *z*) and (*t*′, *x*′, *y*′, *z*′) are the
coordinates of an event in two frames with the origins coinciding
at *t*=*t*′=0, where the primed frame is seen from the unprimed frame as
moving with speed *v* along the *x*-axis, where *c* is the [[speed of
light]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light),
and {\\textstyle \\gamma =\\left({\\sqrt {1-{\\frac
{v\^{2}}{c\^{2}}}}}\\right)\^{-1}} is the [[Lorentz
factor]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_factor). When
speed *v* is much smaller than *c*, the Lorentz factor is negligibly
different from 1, but as *v* approaches *c*, {\\displaystyle \\gamma
} grows without bound. The value of *v* must be smaller than *c* for the
transformation to make sense.

A \"stationary\" observer in frame *F* defines events with

velocity *v* relative to *F*, and an observer in this \"moving\"
frame *F*′ defines events using the coordinates *t*′, *x*′, *y*′, *z*′.

The coordinate axes in each frame are parallel (the *x* and *x*′ axes
are parallel, the *y* and *y*′ axes are parallel, and
the *z* and *z*′ axes are parallel), remain mutually perpendicular, and
relative motion is along the coincident *xx′* axes. At *t* = *t*′ = 0,
the origins of both coordinate systems are the same, (*x, y, z*) =
(*x*′, *y*′, *z*′) = (0, 0, 0). **In other words, the times and
positions are coincident at this event.** If all these hold, then the
coordinate systems are said to be in **standard configuration**,
or **synchronized**.

If an observer in *F* records an event *t*, *x*, *y*, *z*, then an
observer in *F*′ records the *same* event with coordinates. where *v* is
the relative velocity between frames in the *x*-direction, *c* is
the [[speed of
light]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), and
(lowercase [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma)) is the [Lorentz
factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_factor). Here, *v* is
the [*parameter*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameter) of the
transformation, for a given boost it is a constant number, but can take
a continuous range of values. In the setup used here, positive relative
velocity *v* \> 0 is motion along the positive directions of
the *xx*′ axes, zero relative velocity *v* = 0 is no relative motion,
while negative relative velocity *v* \< 0 is relative motion along the
negative directions of the *xx*′ axes. The magnitude of relative
velocity *v* cannot equal or exceed *c*, so only subluminal
speeds −*c* \< *v* \< *c* are allowed. The corresponding range
of *γ* is 1 ≤ *γ* \< ∞.

As an [active
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_transformation), an
observer in F′ notices the coordinates of the event to be \"boosted\" in
the negative directions of the *xx*′ axes, because of the −*v* in the
transformations. This has the equivalent effect of the *coordinate
system* F′ boosted in the positive directions of the *xx*′ axes, while
the event does not change and is simply represented in another
coordinate system, a [passive
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_transformation).

The inverse relations (*t*, *x*, *y*, *z* in terms
of *t*′, *x*′, *y*′, *z*′) can be found by algebraically solving the
original set of equations. A more efficient way is to use physical
principles. Here *F*′ is the \"stationary\" frame while *F* is the
\"moving\" frame. According to the principle of relativity, there is no
privileged frame of reference, so the transformations
from *F*′ to *F* must take exactly the same form as the transformations
from *F* to *F*′. The only difference is *F* moves with
velocity −*v* relative to *F*′ (i.e., the relative velocity has the same
magnitude but is oppositely directed). Thus, if an observer
in *F*′ notes an event *t*′, *x*′, *y*′, *z*′, then an observer
in *F* notes the *same* event with coordinates.

A critical requirement of the Lorentz transformations is the invariance
of the speed of light, a fact used in their derivation, and contained in
the transformations themselves. If in *F* the equation for a pulse of
light along the *x* direction is *x* = *ct*, then in *F*′ the Lorentz
transformations give *x*′ = *ct*′, and vice versa, for
any −*c* \< *v* \< *c*..

**ACTIVE and PASSIVE TRANSFORMATION:**

In [analytic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry),
spatial transformations in the [3-dimensional Euclidean
space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-dimensional_Euclidean_space) {\\displaystyle
\\mathbb {R} \^{3}} are distinguished into **active** or **alibi
transformations**, and **passive** or **alias transformations**.
An **active
transformation**[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_and_passive_transformation#cite_note-1) is
a [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_(mathematics)) which
actually changes the physical position (alibi, elsewhere) of a point,
or [rigid body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body), which can be
defined in the absence of a [coordinate
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_system); whereas
a **passive
transformation**[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_and_passive_transformation#cite_note-2) is
merely a change in the coordinate system in which the object is
described (alias, other name) (change of coordinate map, or [change of
basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_of_basis)).
By *transformation*, [mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematician) usually
refer to active transformations,
while [physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) and [engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer) could
mean either. Both types of transformation can be represented by a
combination of
a [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_(geometry)) and
a [linear
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation).

{v\^{2}}{c\^{2}}}}}}}

Put differently, a *passive* transformation refers to description of
the *same* object in two different coordinate
systems.[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_and_passive_transformation#cite_note-Davidson-3) On
the other hand, an *active transformation* is a transformation of one or
more objects with respect to the same coordinate system.

Historically, the transformations were the result of attempts by Lorentz

**Galilean Transformation:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), a **Galilean
transformation** is used to transform between the coordinates of
two [reference
frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_frames) which differ
only by constant relative motion within the constructs of [Newtonian
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_physics). These
transformations together with spatial rotations and translations in
space and time form the **inhomogeneous Galilean group** (assumed
throughout below). Without the translations in space and time the group
is the **homogeneous Galilean group**. The Galilean group is the [group
of motions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_motions) of [Galilean
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_relativity) acting on
the four dimensions of space and time, forming the **Galilean
geometry**. This is the [passive
transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_and_passive_transformation) point
of view. In [special
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity) the
homogenous and inhomogenous Galilean transformations are, respectively,
replaced by the [Lorentz
transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_transformations) and Poincaré
transformations; conversely, the [group
contraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_contraction) in
the [classical
limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit) *c* → ∞ of
Poincaré transformations yields Galilean transformations.

**Classical Limit:**

The **classical limit** or **correspondence limit** is the ability of
a [physical
theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics) to
approximate or \"recover\" [classical
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics) when
considered over special values of its
parameters.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_limit#cite_note-1) The
classical limit is used with physical theories that predict
non-classical behavior.

**Projectile:**

A **projectile** is an object that is propelled by the application of an
external [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) and then moves
freely under the influence
of [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) and [air
resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_resistance).^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile#cite_note-2)^ Although
any objects in motion through space are projectiles, they are commonly
found
in [warfare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warfare) and [sports](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport) (for
example, a
thrown [baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_(ball)),
kicked [football](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_(association_football)),
fired [bullet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet),
shot [arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow), stone released
from [catapult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catapult)).^[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile#cite_note-4)^

In [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics) mathematical [equations
of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) are used
to analyze
projectile [trajectories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) through
launch, [flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight),
and [impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_ballistics).

In projectile motion, the most important force applied to the
'projectile' is the propelling force, in this case the propelling forces
are the muscles that act upon the ball to make it move, and the stronger
the force applied, the more propelling force, which means the projectile
(the ball) will travel farther.
See [pitching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_(baseball)), [bowling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling).

**[kinetic energy
weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_weapon):**

A [kinetic energy
weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy_weapon) (also known
as kinetic weapon, kinetic energy warhead, kinetic warhead, kinetic
projectile, kinetic kill vehicle) is
a [weapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapon) based solely on a
projectile\'s [kinetic
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) instead of
an [explosive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive) or any other
kind of [payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload).

The term **Hit-to-kill**, or kinetic kill, is also used in the
military [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace) field to
describe kinetic energy weapons. It has been used primarily in
the [anti-ballistic
missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-ballistic_missile) (ABM)
and [anti-satellite
weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-satellite_weapon) (ASAT)
area, but some modern [anti-aircraft
missiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-aircraft_missile) are also
hit-to-kill. Hit-to-kill systems are part of the wider class of kinetic
projectiles, a class that has widespread use in
the [anti-tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-tank_warfare) field.

Typical kinetic energy weapons are blunt projectiles such
as [rocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_(geology)) and [round
shots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_shot), pointed ones such
as [arrows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrow), and somewhat pointed
ones such as [bullets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet).

**Kinetic Energy:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), the **kinetic
energy** of an object is
the [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) that it possesses due
to
its [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_(physics)).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy#cite_note-1) It
is defined as
the [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_(physics)) needed to
accelerate a body of a given mass from rest to its
stated [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity). Having gained
this energy during
its [acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration), the body
maintains this kinetic energy unless its speed changes. **The same
amount of work is done by the body when decelerating from its current
speed to a state of rest.** Formally, a kinetic energy is any term in a
system\'s Lagrangian which includes
a [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivative) with
respect
to [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time). ^[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy#cite_note-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy#cite_note-3)^

In [classical
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics), the
kinetic energy of a non-rotating object
of [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) *m* traveling at
a [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed) *v* is 
$\frac{1}{2}*m*v^{2}${\\textstyle {\\frac {1}{2}}mv\^{2}}.

**Work:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), **work** is
the [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) transferred to or
from an object via the application of force along a displacement. **In
its simplest form, it is often represented as the product
of** [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) and [displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_(vector)).
A **force is said to do positive work if (when applied) it has a
component in the direction of the displacement of the point of
application.** A force does negative work if it has a component opposite
to the direction of the displacement at the point of application of the
force.

# Force

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), a **force** is an
influence that can change
the [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion) of
an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_object). A force can
cause an object with [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) to
change its [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) (e.g.
moving from a [state of
rest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_first_law)), i.e.,
to [accelerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accelerate). Force can
also be described intuitively as a push or a pull. A force has
both [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector#Length) and [direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direction_(geometry,_geography)),
making it
a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_(geometric)) quantity.
It is measured in the [SI
unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_unit) of [newton
(N)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_(unit)). Force is represented
by the symbol **F** (formerly **P**).

If the mass of the object is constant, this law implies that
the [acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration) of an
object is
directly [proportional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportionality_(mathematics)) to
the net force acting on the object, is in the direction of the net
force, and is inversely proportional to
the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of the object.

Concepts related to force
include: [thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust), which increases
the velocity of an
object; [drag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_(physics)), which
decreases the velocity of an object;
and [torque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque), which
produces [changes in rotational
speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_acceleration) of an object.
In an extended body, each part usually applies forces on the adjacent
parts; the distribution of such forces through the body is the
internal [mechanical
stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_(mechanics)).

# Thrust

**Thrust** is
a [reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_(physics)) [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_(physics)) described
quantitatively by [Newton\'s third
law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_third_law). When a system
expels
or [accelerates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration) [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) in
one direction, the accelerated mass will cause a force of
equal [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_(vector)) but
opposite direction to be applied to that
system.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thrust#cite_note-1) The
force applied on a surface in a direction perpendicular
or [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector) to the surface
is also called thrust. Force, and thus thrust, is measured using
the [International System of
Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI)
in [newtons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_(unit)) (symbol: N),
and represents the amount needed to accelerate 1 kilogram of mass at the
rate of 1 [meter per second per
second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_per_second_squared).
In [mechanical
engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineering),
force [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) to the main
load (such as in parallel [helical
gears](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helical_gear)) is referred to
as [static thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statics).

# Drag (physics)

In [fluid
dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics), **drag** (sometimes
called **air resistance**, a type
of [friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction), or **fluid
resistance**, another type of friction or fluid friction) is
a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) acting opposite to the
relative motion of any object moving with respect to a surrounding
fluid.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_(physics)#cite_note-1) This
can exist between two fluid layers (or surfaces) or between a fluid and
a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) surface. Unlike other
resistive forces, such as dry friction, which are nearly independent of
velocity, the **drag force** depends on
velocity.^[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_(physics)#cite_note-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_(physics)#cite_note-NASAdrag-3)^

**Drag force is proportional to the velocity for low-speed flow and the
squared velocity for high speed flow, where the distinction between low
and high speed is measured by the [Reynolds
number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reynolds_number).** Even though
the ultimate cause of drag is viscous friction, turbulent drag is
independent
of [viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscosity).[^\[4\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drag_(physics)#cite_note-4)

Drag forces always tend to decrease fluid velocity relative to the solid
object in the fluid\'s path.

**Fluid Dynamics:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), **fluid
dynamics** is a subdiscipline of [fluid
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_mechanics) that describes
the **flow** of [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid)---[liquids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) and [gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas).
It has several subdisciplines,
including [*aerodynamics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerodynamics) (the
study of air and other gases in motion) and **hydrodynamics** (the study
of liquids in motion). Fluid dynamics has a wide range of applications,
including
calculating [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) and [moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_(physics)) on [aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft),
determining the [mass flow
rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_flow_rate) of [petroleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroleum) through [pipelines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipeline_transport), [predicting
weather patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_forecasting),
understanding [nebulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula) in [interstellar
space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_space) and [modelling
fission weapon
detonation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon_design).

**Torque:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), **torque** is
the rotational equivalent of
linear [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque#cite_note-1) It
is also referred to as the **moment**, **moment of force**, **rotational
force** or **turning effect**, depending on the field of study. It
represents the capability of a force to produce change in the rotational
motion of the body. The concept originated with the studies
by [Archimedes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes) of the usage
of [levers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lever), which is reflected in
his famous quote: \"*Give me a lever and a place to stand and I will
move the Earth*\". **Just as a linear force is a push or a pull, a
torque can be thought of as a twist to an object around a specific
axis.** **Torque is defined as the product of the magnitude of the force
and the perpendicular distance** of the [line of
action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_action) of a force from
the [axis of
rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_around_a_fixed_axis).
The symbol for torque is typically tau{\\displaystyle {\\boldsymbol
{\\tau }}}tquta, the lowercase [Greek
letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) [*tau*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau).
When being referred to
as [moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_(physics)) of force, it
is commonly denoted by *M*.

**Line of Action:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), the **line of
action** (also called **line of application**) of
a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) *(F)* is a geometric
representation of how the force is applied. It is
the [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_(mathematics)) through the
point at which the force is applied in the same direction as
the [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_(mathematics_and_physics)) *F*→.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_action#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_of_action#cite_note-2)^

The concept is essential, for instance, for understanding the [net
effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_force) of multiple forces
applied to a [body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_object). For
example, if two forces of
equal [magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_(mathematics)) act
upon a [rigid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_rigidity) body
along the same line of action but in opposite directions, they cancel
and have no net effect. But if, instead, their lines of action are not
identical, but
merely [parallel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_(geometry)),
then their effect is to create
a [moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_(physics)) on the body,
which tends
to [rotate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_around_a_fixed_axis) it.

**Rotation around a fixed axis:**

**Rotation around a fixed axis** is a special case
of [rotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation) motion. The
fixed-[axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate_system) hypothesis
excludes the possibility of an axis changing its orientation and cannot
describe such phenomena
as [wobbling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutation) or [precession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precession).
According to [Euler\'s rotation
theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler%27s_rotation_theorem),
simultaneous rotation along a number of stationary axes at the same time
is impossible; if two rotations are forced at the same time, a new axis
of rotation will appear.

This article assumes that the rotation is also stable, such that
no [torque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torque) is required to keep it
going.
The [kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematics) and [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_(mechanics)) of
rotation around a fixed axis of a rigid body are mathematically much
simpler than those for [free rotation of a rigid
body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body_dynamics#Rigid-body_angular_momentum);
they are entirely analogous to those of [linear
motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_motion) along a single
fixed direction, which is not true for *free rotation of a rigid body*

**Rigid-Body Dynamics:**

In the [physical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) science
of [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_(mechanics)), **rigid-body
dynamics** studies the movement
of [systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) of
interconnected [bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_object) under
the action of external [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force).
The assumption that the bodies are *rigid* (i.e. they do
not [deform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_(physics)) under
the action of applied forces) simplifies analysis, by reducing the
parameters that describe the configuration of the system to the
translation and rotation of [reference
frames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference) attached to
each
body.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body_dynamics#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_body_dynamics#cite_note-2)^ This
excludes bodies that
display [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid),
highly [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_(physics)),
and [plastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasticity_(physics)) behavior.

The dynamics of a rigid body system is described by the laws
of [kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinematics) and by the
application of Newton\'s second law
([kinetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion)) or
their derivative form, Lagrangian mechanics. The solution of these
equations of motion provides a description of the position, the motion
and the acceleration of the individual components of the system, and
overall the system itself, as a [function of
time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-variant_system). The
formulation and solution of rigid body dynamics is an important tool in
the computer simulation of [mechanical
systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_system).

# Stationary-action principle

The **stationary-action principle** -- also known as the **principle of
least action** -- is a [variational
principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_principle) that,
when applied to
the [*action*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_(physics)) of
a [mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics) system, yields
the [equations of
motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) for that
system. The principle states that the trajectories (i.e. the solutions
of the equations of motion) are [*stationary
points*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_point) of the
system\'s *action functional*. The term \"least action\" is a historical
misnomer since the principle has no minimality requirement: the value of
the action functional need not be minimal (even locally) on the
trajectories.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary-action_principle#cite_note-:0-1) Least
action refers to the absolute value of the action functional being
minimized.

# Conservative force

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), a **conservative
force** is a [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) with the
property that the
total [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work_(physics)) done in
moving a particle between two points is independent of the path
taken.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_force#cite_note-1) Equivalently,
if a particle travels in a closed loop, the total work done (the sum of
the force acting along the path multiplied by
the [displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_(geometry)))
by a conservative force is
zero.[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_force#cite_note-Hand-2)

A **conservative force** depends only on the position of the object. If
a force is conservative, it is possible to assign a numerical value for
the [potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_potential) at any
point and conversely, when an object moves from one location to another,
the force changes the [potential
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy) of the object by
an amount that does not depend on the path taken, contributing to
the [mechanical
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_energy) and the
overall [conservation of
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy). **If the
force is not conservative, then defining a scalar potential is not
possible, because taking different paths would lead to conflicting
potential differences between the start and end points.**

[Gravitational force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation) is an
example of a conservative force; Other examples of conservative forces
are: [force in elastic
spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke%27s_law), [electrostatic
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_force) between two
electric charges, and [magnetic
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_force) between two
magnetic poles. The last two forces are called central forces as they
act along the line joining the centres of two charged/magnetized bodies.
A central force is conservative if and only if it is spherically
symmetric.

# Scalar potential

In [mathematical
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_physics), **scalar
potential**, simply stated, describes the situation where the difference
in **the [potential
energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy) of an object
in two different positions depends only on the positions, not upon the
path taken by the object in traveling from one position to the other.**
It is a scalar field in three-space: a directionless value (scalar) that
depends only on its location. A familiar example is potential energy due
to gravity.

# Potential Energy

In physics, **potential energy** is
the [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) held by an object
because of its position relative to other objects, stresses within
itself, its electric charge, or other
factors.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy#cite_note-2)^

Common types of potential energy include the [gravitational potential
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_potential_energy) of
an object, the [elastic potential
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_potential_energy) of an
extended spring, and the [electric potential
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_potential_energy) of
an [electric charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge) in
an [electric field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field). The
unit for energy in the [International System of
Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI)
is the [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule), which has the
symbol J.

The term *potential energy* was introduced by the 19th-century Scottish
engineer and physicist [William
Rankine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Rankine),^[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy#cite_note-4)^ although
it has links to Greek
philosopher [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)\'s
concept
of [potentiality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiality_and_Actuality).
Potential energy is associated with forces that act on a body in a way
that the total work done by these forces on the body depends only on the
initial and final positions of the body in space. These forces, that are
called [*conservative
forces*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_force), can be
represented at every point in space by vectors expressed as gradients of
a certain scalar function called *potential*.

Since the work of potential forces acting on a body that moves from a
start to an end position is determined only by these two positions and
does not depend on the trajectory of the body, there is a function known
as *potential* that can be evaluated at the two positions to determine
this work.

There are various types of potential energy, each associated with a
particular type of force. For example, the work of
an [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_(physics)) force
is called elastic potential energy; work of the gravitational force is
called gravitational potential energy; work of the [Coulomb
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_force) is called [electric
potential
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_potential_energy); work
of the [strong nuclear
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_nuclear_force) or [weak
nuclear force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_nuclear_force) acting
on
the [baryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon) [charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_(physics)) is
called nuclear potential energy; work of [intermolecular
forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermolecular_forces) is called
intermolecular potential energy. Chemical potential energy, such as the
energy stored in [fossil
fuels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossil_fuels), is the work of the
Coulomb force during rearrangement of configurations of electrons and
nuclei in atoms and molecules. Thermal energy usually has two
components: the kinetic energy of random motions of particles and the
potential energy of their configuration.

# Action (physics)

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), **action** is a
numerical value describing how a [physical
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) has [changed over
time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_(physics)). Action is
significant because the [equations of
motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) of the system
can be derived through the [principle of stationary
action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_stationary_action).  

In the simple case of a single particle moving with a specified
velocity, the action is
the [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum) of the particle
times the distance it moves, [added
up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_(mathematics)) along its
path, or equivalently, twice its [kinetic
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) times the length
of time for which it has that amount of energy, added up over the period
of time under consideration. For more complicated systems, all such
quantities are added together.

More formally, action is a [mathematical
functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_(mathematics)) which
takes the [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory), also
called *path* or *history*, of the system as its argument and has
a [real number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number) as its
result. Generally, the action takes different values for different
paths.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_(physics)#cite_note-mcgraw1-1) Action
has [dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional_analysis) of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) × [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) or [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum) × [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length),
and its [SI
unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_unit) is [joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule)-second
(like the [Planck
constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant) *h*).[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_(physics)#cite_note-2)

# In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry), there are two closely related [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space), usually [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_space) but in general of any finite dimension. Position space (also real space or [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_system) space) is the set of all [*position vectors*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector) r in space, and has [dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensional_analysis) of [length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length); a position vector defines a point in space. # [Newton\'s second law of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_laws_of_motion#Newton's_second_law) states that the rate of change of a body\'s momentum is equal to the net force acting on it. Momentum depends on the [frame of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference), but in any inertial frame it is a *conserved* quantity, meaning that if a [closed system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_system) is not affected by external forces, its total linear momentum does not change. Momentum is also conserved in [special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity) (with a modified formula) and, in a modified form, in [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics), [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics), [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory), and [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity). It is an expression of one of the fundamental symmetries of space and time: [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry).

# Gravitational energy

**Gravitational energy** or **gravitational potential energy** is
the [potential
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potential_energy) a [massive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) object
has in relation to another massive object due
to [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity). It is the potential
energy associated with the [gravitational
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field), which is
released (converted into [kinetic
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy)) when the
objects [fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_fall) towards each
other. Gravitational potential energy increases when two objects are
brought further apart.

For two pairwise interacting point particles, the gravitational
potential energy {\\displaystyle U}UUUU is given by:

$$U = \  - \frac{GMm}{R}$$

where  $M$ {\\displaystyle M}MM$M$and $m${\\displaystyle m}are the
masses of the two particles, $R$ {\\displaystyle R} $R$is the distance
between them, and {\\displaystyle G}$G\ G$is the [gravitational
constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_energy#cite_note-gpe-1)

Close to the Earth\'s surface, the gravitational field is approximately
constant, and the gravitational potential energy of an object reduces
to:

$$U = m*g*h$$

where {\\displaystyle m} m is the object\'s mass, g {\\textstyle
g={GM\_{\\oplus }}/{R\_{\\oplus }\^{2}}} gg is the [gravity of
Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_of_Earth), and
h {\\displaystyle h}h nhb is the height of the object\'s [center of

reference

**Classical Mechanics:**

**Classical
mechanics**[^\[note\ 1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics#cite_note-3) is
a [physical
theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics) describing
the [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion) of [macroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic) objects,
from [projectiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectile) to parts
of [machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_(mechanical)),
and [astronomical
objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_objects), such
as [spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacecraft), [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planets), [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star),
and [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxies). **For objects
governed by classical mechanics, if the present state is known, it is
possible to predict how it will move in the future (determinism), and
how it has moved in the past (reversibility)**.

The earliest development of classical mechanics is often referred to
as [Newtonian
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_mechanics). It
consists of the physical concepts based on foundational works of
Sir [Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton), and the
mathematical methods invented by [Gottfried Wilhelm
Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Wilhelm_Leibniz), [Joseph-Louis
Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph-Louis_Lagrange), [Leonhard
Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonhard_Euler), and other
contemporaries, in the 17th century to describe the motion
of [bodies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_body) under the
influence of a system of [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force).
Later, more abstract methods were developed, leading to the
reformulations of classical mechanics known as Lagrangian
mechanics and [Hamiltonian
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics). These
advances, made predominantly in the 18th and 19th centuries, extend
substantially beyond earlier works, particularly through their use
of [analytical
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_mechanics). They
are, with some modification, also used in all areas of modern physics.

Classical mechanics provides extremely accurate results when studying
large objects that are not extremely massive and speeds not approaching
the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). When
the objects being examined have about the size of an atom diameter, it
becomes necessary to introduce the other major sub-field
of [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics): [quantum
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics). To describe
velocities that are not small compared to the speed of light, [special
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity) is needed.
In cases where objects become extremely massive, [general
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity) becomes
applicable. However, a number of modern sources do include [relativistic
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_mechanics) in
classical physics, which in their view represents classical mechanics in
its most developed and accurate form. extremely massive, [general
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity) becomes
applicable. However, a number of modern sources do include [relativistic
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_mechanics) in

# Metaphysics

**Metaphysics** is the branch
of [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) that studies
the fundamental nature of reality**, the first principles of being,
identity and change, space and time, causality, necessity, and
possibility**.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics#cite_note-1) **It
includes questions about the nature**
of [consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consciousness) and
**the relationship
between** [mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind) and [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter),
between [substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substance_theory) and [attribute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_(philosophy)),
and between [potentiality and
actuality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiality_and_actuality).[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics#cite_note-2) The
word \"metaphysics\" comes from two Greek words that, together,
literally mean \"after or behind or among \[the study of\] the
natural\". It has been suggested that the term might have been coined by
a first century CE editor who assembled various small selections
of [Aristotle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle)\'s works into
the treatise we now know by the
name [*Metaphysics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics_(Aristotle)) (μετὰ
τὰ φυσικά, *meta ta physika*, lit. \'after
the [*Physics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_(Aristotle)) \',
another of Aristotle\'s
works).[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics#cite_note-Stanford.Enc.Philosophy-3)

Metaphysics studies questions related to what it is for something to
exist and what types of existence there are. Metaphysics seeks to
answer, in an abstract and fully general manner, the questions of

1.  What *there is*

2.  What it is *like*

**[Metaphysical naturalism:]{.underline}**

**Metaphysical naturalism** (also called **ontological
naturalism**, **philosophical naturalism** and **antisupernaturalism**)
is a philosophical worldview which holds that there is nothing
but [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural) elements,
principles, and relations of the kind studied by the [natural
sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_sciences). [Methodological
naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methodological_naturalism) is
a philosophical basis for science, for which metaphysical naturalism
provides only one
possible [ontological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) foundation.
Broadly, the corresponding theological perspective is [religious
naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_naturalism) or [spiritual
naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_naturalism). More
specifically, metaphysical naturalism rejects
the [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural) concepts
and explanations that are part of
many [religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religions).

# Abstract and concrete

In [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), the
distinction between **abstract** and **concrete** refers to a divide
between two types of entities. Many philosophers hold that this
difference has fundamental metaphysical significance. Examples of
concrete objects
include [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant), [human
beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human) and [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet) while
things
like [numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number), [sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_(mathematics)) and [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition) are **abstract
objects**.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-Rosen-1) There
is no
general [consensus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consensus_decision-making) as
to what the characteristic marks of concreteness and abstractness are.
Popular suggestions include defining the distinction in terms of the
difference between (1) existence inside or outside space-time, (2)
having causes and effects or not, (3) having contingent or necessary
existence, (4) being particular or universal, and (5) belonging to
either the physical or the mental realm or to
neither.^[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-A_Glossary_of_Literary_Terms-4)^ Despite
this diversity of views, there is broad agreement concerning most
objects as to whether they are abstract or
concrete.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-Rosen-1) So
under most interpretations, all these views would agree that, for
example, plants are concrete objects while numbers are abstract objects.

Abstract objects are most commonly used
in [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) and [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics).
They are sometimes called ***abstracta*** in contrast to *concreta*. The
term *abstract object* is said to have been coined by [Willard Van Orman
Quine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Van_Orman_Quine).[^\[5\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-5) [Abstract
object theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_object_theory) is
a discipline that studies the nature and role of abstract objects. It
holds that properties can be related to objects in two ways: through
exemplification and through encoding. Concrete objects exemplify their
properties while abstract objects merely encode them. This approach is
also known as the [dual copula
strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_copula_strategy).[^\[6\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_and_concrete#cite_note-FOOTNOTEZalta198333-6)

# Naturalism (philosophy)

In [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), **naturalism** is
the idea or belief that
only [natural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law) laws and
forces (as opposed
to [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural) ones)
operate in
the [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(philosophy)#cite_note-1)

"Naturalism is not so much a special system as a point of view or
tendency common to a number of philosophical and religious systems; not
so much a well-defined set of positive and negative doctrines as an
attitude or spirit pervading and influencing many doctrines. As the name
implies, this tendency consists essentially in looking upon nature as
the one original and fundamental source of all that exists, and in
attempting to explain everything in terms of nature. Either the limits
of nature are also the limits of existing reality, or at least the first
cause, [[if its existence is found
necessary]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmological_argument),
has nothing to do with the working of natural agencies. All events,
therefore, find their adequate explanation within nature itself. But, as
the terms nature and natural are themselves used in more than one sense,
the term naturalism is also far from having one fixed meaning."

--- [*[Dubray
1911]{.underline}*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(philosophy)#CITEREFDubray1911)

# Supernatural

The **supernatural** are phenomena or entities that are not subject to
the [laws of nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_law). The
term is derived from Medieval Latin *supernaturalis*, from
Latin *super-* (above, beyond, or outside of)
+ *natura* (nature)[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural#cite_note-merriam-1) Though
the corollary term \"nature\", has had multiple meanings since the
ancient world, the term \"supernatural\" emerged in the [Middle
Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages)[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural#cite_note-Bartlett-2) and
did not exist in the ancient
world.[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural#cite_note-Oxford-3)

The supernatural is featured
in [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore) and [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion) contexts,[^\[4\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural#cite_note-4) but
can also feature as an explanation in more secular contexts, as in the
cases of [superstitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstition) or
belief in
the [paranormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranormal).[^\[5\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural#cite_note-Halman_2010-5) The
term is attributed to [non-physical
entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-physical_entity), such
as [angels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel), [demons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demon), [gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gods),
and [spirits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost). It also includes
claimed abilities embodied in or provided by such beings,
including [magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_(supernatural)), [telekinesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telekinesis), [levitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levitation_(paranormal)), [precognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precognition),
and [extrasensory
perception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extrasensory_perception).

The philosophy
of [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(philosophy)) contends
that nothing exists beyond the natural world, and as such approaches
supernatural claims
with [skepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skepticism).[^\[6\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural#cite_note-6)

# Wave propagation

**Wave propagation** is any of the ways in
which [waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_(physics)) travel.
Single wave propagation can be calculated by 2nd order [wave
equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_equation) ([standing
wavefield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_wave)) or 1st
order [one-way wave
equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-way_wave_equation).

With respect to the direction of
the [oscillation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation) relative to
the propagation direction, we can distinguish between [longitudinal
wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave) and [transverse
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_wave).

For [electromagnetic
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave), propagation
may occur in a [vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum) as well as
in the material medium. Other wave types cannot propagate through a
vacuum and need a [transmission
medium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transmission_medium) to exist.

# Longitudinal wave

**Longitudinal
waves** are [waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave) in which
the [vibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibration) of the medium
is parallel (\"along\") to the direction the wave travels and
displacement of the medium is in the same (or opposite) direction of
the [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation).

[Mechanical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_wave) longitudinal
waves are also called *compressional* or **compression waves**, because
they
produce [compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compression_(physics)) and [rarefaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rarefaction) when
traveling through a medium, and **pressure waves**, because they produce
increases and decreases
in [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure). A wave along the
length of a
stretched [Slinky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slinky) toy, where the
distance between coils increases and decreases, is a good visualization.
Real-world examples include sound waves
([vibrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibration) in pressure, a
particle of displacement, and particle velocity propagated in
an [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_(physics)) medium)
and seismic [P-waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-wave) (created by
earthquakes and explosions).

The other main type of wave is the [transverse
wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transverse_wave), in which the
displacements of the medium are at right angles to the direction of
propagation. Transverse waves, for instance, describe *some* bulk sound
waves in [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) materials (but not
in [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluids)); these are also
called
\"[shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shearing_(physics)) waves\" to
differentiate them from the (longitudinal) pressure waves that these
materials also support.

# Transverse wave

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), a **transverse
wave** is a [wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave) whose
oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of the wave\'s advance.
This is in contrast to a [longitudinal
wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave) which travels in
the direction of its oscillations. [Water
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wave) are an example of
transverse wave.

A simple example is given by the waves that can be created on a
horizontal length of string by anchoring one end and moving the other
end up and down. Another example is the waves that are created on the
membrane of a [drum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum). The waves
propagate in directions that are parallel to the membrane plane, but the
membrane itself gets displaced up and down, perpendicular to that
plane. [Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) is another example
of a transverse wave, where the oscillations are
the [electric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field) and [magnetic
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field), which point at
right angles to the ideal light rays that describe the direction of
propagation.

# Magnetic field

A **magnetic field** is a [vector
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field) that describes the
magnetic influence on moving [electric
charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge), [electric
currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_currents),^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-feynman-1): ch1 [\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-2)^ and
magnetic materials. A moving charge in a magnetic field experiences a
force perpendicular to its own velocity and to the magnetic
field.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-feynman-1): ch13 [\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-Purcell3ed-3): 278 ^ A [permanent
magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_magnet)\'s magnetic
field pulls on [ferromagnetic
materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic_material) such
as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), and attracts or repels
other magnets. In addition, a magnetic field that varies with location
will exert a force on a range of non-magnetic materials by affecting the
motion of their outer atomic electrons. Magnetic fields surround
magnetized materials, and are created by electric currents such as those
used in [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet),
and by [electric
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field) varying in time.
Since both strength and direction of a magnetic field may vary with
location, it is described mathematically by
a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_(mathematics)) assigning
a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_(mathematics_and_physics)) to
each point of space, called a [vector
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field).

# Photon

A **photon** (from [Ancient
Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_language) [φῶς](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%86%E1%BF%B6%CF%82#Ancient_Greek)*, *[φωτός](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%CF%86%CF%89%CF%84%CF%8C%CF%82#Ancient_Greek)* (phôs,
phōtós)* \'light\') is an [elementary
particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) that is
a [quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum) of
the [electromagnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field),
including [electromagnetic
radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) such
as [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) and [radio
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave), and the [force
carrier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force_carrier) for
the [electromagnetic
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_force). Photons
are [massless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle),[^\[a\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-2) so
they always move at the [speed of light in
vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light), 299792458 m/s (or
about 186,282 mi/s). The photon belongs to the class
of [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson).

As with other elementary particles, photons are best explained
by [quantum
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics) and
exhibit [wave--particle
duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave%E2%80%93particle_duality),
their behavior featuring properties of
both [waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave) and [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle).[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-3) The
modern photon concept originated during the first two decades of the
20th century with the work of [Albert
Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein), who built upon
the research of [Max Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck).
While trying to explain
how [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter) and electromagnetic
radiation could be in [thermal
equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_equilibrium) with one
another, Planck proposed that the energy stored within
a [material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material) object should be
regarded as composed of
an [integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer) number of discrete,
equal-sized parts. To explain the [photoelectric
effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect), Einstein
introduced the idea that light itself is made of discrete units of
energy. In 1926, [Gilbert N.
Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilbert_N._Lewis) popularized the
term *photon* for these energy
units.^[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-:0-4)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-5)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-kragh-6)^ Subsequently,
many other experiments validated Einstein\'s
approach.^[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-compton-lecture-7)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-8)[\[8\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon#cite_note-9)^

# In the [Standard Model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Model) of [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics), photons and other elementary particles are described as a necessary consequence of physical laws having a certain [symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_(physics)) at every point in [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime). The intrinsic properties of particles, such as [charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge), [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_mass), and [spin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_(physics)), are determined by [gauge symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_symmetry). The photon concept has led to momentous advances in experimental and theoretical physics, including [lasers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser), [Bose--Einstein condensation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose%E2%80%93Einstein_condensation), [quantum field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_field_theory), and the [probabilistic interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude) of quantum mechanics. It has been applied to [photochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photochemistry), [high-resolution microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-photon_excitation_microscopy), and [measurements of molecular distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorescence_resonance_energy_transfer). Moreover, photons have been studied as elements of [quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computer), and for applications in [optical imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_imaging) and [optical communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_communication) such as [quantum cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_cryptography).

# Field (physics) \[Physical Field\]

In physics, a **field** is a [physical
quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity), represented
by
a [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_(mathematics)), [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_(mathematics_and_physics)),
or [tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor), that has a value for
each [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_(geometry)) in [space
and
time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime).^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-Gribbin-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-Feynman2Ch1S2-2)[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-3)^ For
example, on a weather map, the
surface [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature) is
described by assigning
a [number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_number) to each point on
the map; the temperature can be considered at a certain point in time or
over some interval of time, to study the dynamics of temperature change.
A surface wind
map,[^\[4\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-4) assigning
an [arrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_(mathematics_and_physics)) to
each point on a map that describes the wind [speed and
direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity) at that point, is an
example of a [vector field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field),
i.e. a 1-dimensional (rank-1) tensor field. Field theories, mathematical
descriptions of how field values change in space and time, are
ubiquitous in physics. For instance, the [electric
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field) is another rank-1
tensor field,
while [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics) can
be formulated in terms of [two interacting vector
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_descriptions_of_the_electromagnetic_field) at
each point in spacetime, or as a [single-rank
2-tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_formulation_of_classical_electromagnetism) field.^[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-Feynman102-6)[\[7\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-Feynman204-7)^

In the modern framework of the [quantum theory of
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_theory_of_fields), even
without referring to a test particle, a field occupies space, contains
energy, and its presence precludes a classical \"true
vacuum\".[^\[8\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-Wheeler-8) This
has led physicists to consider [electromagnetic
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) to be a
physical entity, making the field concept a
supporting [paradigm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradigm) of the
edifice of modern physics. \"The fact that the electromagnetic field can
possess momentum and energy makes it very real \... a particle makes a
field, and a field acts on another particle, and the field has such
familiar properties as energy content and momentum, just as particles
can
have.\"[^\[9\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-Feynman110-9) In
practice, the strength of most fields diminishes with distance,
eventually becoming undetectable. For instance the strength of many
relevant classical fields, such as the gravitational field in [Newton\'s
theory of
gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton%27s_law_of_universal_gravitation) or
the [electrostatic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_field) in classical
electromagnetism, is inversely proportional to the square of the
distance from the source (i.e., they follow [Gauss\'s
law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss%27s_law)).

![](media/image7.png){width="2.6125in" height="1.9590277777777778in"}A
field can be classified as a [scalar
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_field), a [vector
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field), a [spinor
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_field) or a [tensor
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_field) according to whether
the represented physical quantity is
a [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_(physics)),
a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_vector),
a [spinor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor), or
a [tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor), respectively. A field
has a consistent tensorial character wherever it is defined: i.e. a
field cannot be a scalar field somewhere and a vector field somewhere
else. For example,
the [Newtonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newtonian_gravity) [gravitational
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field) is a vector
field: specifying its value at a point in spacetime requires three
numbers, the components of the gravitational field vector at that point.
Moreover, within each category (scalar, vector, tensor), a field can be
either a *classical field* or a *quantum field*, depending on whether it
is characterized by numbers or [quantum
operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_(physics)) respectively.
In this theory an equivalent representation of field is a [field
particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_particle), for instance
a [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson).[^\[10\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)#cite_note-10)

# Electromagnetism

**Electromagnetism** is a branch
of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) involving the study
of the **electromagnetic force**, a type of [physical
interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_interaction) that
occurs between [electrically
charged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge) particles. The
electromagnetic force is carried by [electromagnetic
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) composed
of [electric
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field) and [magnetic
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field), and it is
responsible for [electromagnetic
radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) such
as [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light). It is one of the
four [fundamental
interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction) (commonly
called forces) in [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature),
together with the [strong
interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_interaction),
the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction),
and [gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism#cite_note-1) At
high energy, the weak force and electromagnetic force are unified as a
single [electroweak
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_interaction).

Electromagnetic phenomena are defined in terms of the electromagnetic
force, sometimes called the [Lorentz
force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_force), which includes
both [electricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electricity) and [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) as
different manifestations of the same phenomenon. The electromagnetic
force plays a major role in determining the internal properties of most
objects encountered in daily life. The electromagnetic attraction
between [atomic nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nuclei) and
their
orbital [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) holds [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) together.
Electromagnetic forces are responsible for the [chemical
bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond) between atoms which
create [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule),
and [intermolecular
forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermolecular_force). The
electromagnetic force governs all chemical processes, which arise from
interactions between
the [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) of
neighboring atoms. Electromagnetism is very widely used in modern
technology, and electromagnetic theory is the basis of [electric power
engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) and [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) including
digital technology.

There are numerous [mathematical descriptions of the electromagnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_descriptions_of_the_electromagnetic_field).
Most prominently, [Maxwell\'s
equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_equations) describe
how electric and magnetic fields are generated and altered by each other
and by charges and
currents.[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism#cite_note-2)

The theoretical implications of electromagnetism, particularly the
establishment of the speed of light based on properties of the
\"medium\" of propagation
([permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permeability_(electromagnetism)) and [permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permittivity)),
led to the development of [special
relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_relativity) by [Albert
Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) in 1905.

# Electrical conductor

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) and [electrical
engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering),
a **conductor** is an object or type
of [material](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material) that allows the
flow
of [charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge) ([electric
current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current)) in one or more
directions. Materials made
of [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal) are common electrical
conductors. Electric current is generated by the flow of negatively
charged [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), positively
charged [holes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_hole), and
positive or negative [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) in some
cases.

In order for current to flow within a closed [electrical
circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_network), it is not
necessary for one charged particle to travel from the component
producing the current (the [current
source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_source)) to those
consuming it
(the [loads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_load)). Instead,
the charged particle simply needs to nudge its neighbor a finite amount,
who will nudge *its* neighbor, and on and on until a particle is nudged
into the consumer, thus powering it. Essentially what is occurring is a
long chain
of [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum) transfer between
mobile [charge carriers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_carriers);
the Drude model of conduction describes this process more rigorously.
This momentum transfer model makes metal an ideal choice for a
conductor; metals, characteristically, possess a delocalized [sea of
electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_of_electrons) which gives
the electrons enough mobility to collide and thus affect a momentum
transfer.

As discussed above, electrons are the primary mover in metals; however,
other devices such as the
cationic [electrolyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte)(s) of
a [battery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_(electricity)), or the
mobile protons of the [proton
conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_conductor) of a fuel
cell rely on positive charge
carriers. [Insulators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insulator_(electricity)) are
non-conducting materials with few mobile charges that support only
insignificant electric currents.

# Heisenberg\'s Uncertainty Principle

In [quantum mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics),
the **uncertainty principle** (also known as **Heisenberg\'s uncertainty
principle**) is any of a variety of [mathematical
inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_(mathematics))[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_principle#cite_note-Sen2014-1) asserting
a fundamental limit to the accuracy with which the values for certain
pairs of physical quantities of
a [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle), such
as [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_(vector)), *x*,
and [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum), *p*, can be
predicted from [initial
conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_condition).

Such variable pairs are known as [complementary
variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_(physics)) or [canonically
conjugate
variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_coordinates); and,
depending on interpretation, the uncertainty principle limits to what
extent such conjugate properties maintain their approximate meaning, as
the mathematical framework of [quantum
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics) does not
support the notion of simultaneously well-defined conjugate properties
expressed by a single value. The uncertainty principle implies that it
is in general not possible to predict the value of a quantity with
arbitrary certainty, even if all initial conditions are specified.

# Complementarity (physics)

In physics, **complementarity** is a conceptual aspect of [quantum
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics) that [Niels
Bohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bohr) regarded as an essential
feature of the
theory.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_(physics)#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_(physics)#cite_note-Howard_2004-2)^ **The
complementarity principle holds that** objects have certain **pairs of
complementary properties** which **cannot** all **be observed or
measured simultaneously.** An example of **such a pair is position and
momentum.** Bohr considered one of the **foundational truths** of
quantum mechanics to be the fact that **setting up an experiment to
measure one quantity of a pair, for instance the position of
an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron), excludes the
possibility of measuring the other, yet understanding both experiments
is necessary to characterize the object under study.** In Bohr\'s view,
the **behavior of atomic and subatomic objects cannot be separated from
the measuring instruments** that create the context in which the
measured objects behave. **Consequently, there is no \"single picture\"
that unifies the results obtained in these different experimental
contexts, and only the \"totality of the phenomena\" together can
provide a completely informative
description.**[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementarity_(physics)#cite_note-Bohr1996a-3)

# Electric Field

An **electric
field** (sometimes **E-field**[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-1))
is the [physical
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_(physics)) that surrounds
electrically [charged
particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charged_particle) and exerts
force on all other charged particles in the field, either attracting or
repelling
them.[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-2) It
also refers to the physical field for a system of charged
particles.[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-3) Electric
fields originate from electric charges and time-varying [electric
currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current). Electric
fields and magnetic fields are both manifestations of
the [electromagnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), one of the
four [fundamental
interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_interaction) (also
called forces) of nature.

Electric fields are important in many areas
of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), and are exploited
in electrical technology. **In [atomic
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_physics) and [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry),
for instance, the electric field is the attractive force holding
the [atomic
nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) and [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) together
in atoms. It is also the force responsible for [chemical
bonding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bonding) between atoms
that result in [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule).**

The electric field is defined as a [vector
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field) that associates to
each point in space the (electrostatic
or [Coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb%27s_law)) force per
unit of [charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge) exerted
on an infinitesimal positive [test
charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_charge) at rest at that
point.^[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-4)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-5)[\[6\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-Serway-6)^ The [derived
SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_derived_unit) unit for the electric
field is
the [volt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt) per [meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter) (V/m),
which is equal to
the [newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton_(unit)) per [coulomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb) (N/C).[^\[7\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_field#cite_note-7)

# Magnetic Field

A **magnetic field** is a [vector
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_field) that describes the
magnetic influence on moving [electric
charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge), [electric
currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_currents),^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-feynman-1): ch1 [\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-2)^ and
magnetic materials. A moving charge in a magnetic field experiences a
force perpendicular to its own velocity and to the magnetic
field.^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-feynman-1): ch13 [\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field#cite_note-Purcell3ed-3): 278 ^ A [permanent
magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_magnet)\'s magnetic
field pulls on [ferromagnetic
materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic_material) such
as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), and attracts or repels
other magnets. In addition, a magnetic field that varies with location
will exert a force on a range of non-magnetic materials by affecting the
motion of their outer atomic electrons.

# Valence electron

In [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) and [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics),
a **valence electron** is
an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) in the
outer [shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_shell) associated
with an [atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom), and that can
participate in the formation of a [chemical
bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond) if the outer shell is
not closed. In a single [covalent
bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond), both atoms in the
bond contribute one valence electron in order to form a [shared
pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_pair).

The presence of valence electrons can determine
the [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element)\'s [chemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) properties,
such as
its [valence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_(chemistry))---whether
it may bond with other elements and, if so, how readily and with how
many. In this way, a given
element\'s [reactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactivity_(chemistry)) is
highly dependent upon its [electronic
configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration).
For a [main-group
element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Main-group_element), a valence
electron can exist only in the outermost [electron
shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_shell); for a [transition
metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metal), a valence
electron can also be in an inner shell.

An atom with a [closed
shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_shell) of valence electrons
(corresponding to a [noble gas
configuration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noble_gas_configuration))
tends to be [chemically
inert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inert_gases). **Atoms with one or
two valence electrons more than a closed shell are highly reactive due
to the relatively [low
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization_energy) to remove the
extra valence electrons to form a
positive [ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion). An atom with one or
two electrons fewer than a closed shell is reactive due to its tendency
either to gain the missing valence electrons and form a negative ion, or
else to share valence electrons and form a covalent bond.**

Similar to a [core
electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_electron), **a valence
electron has the ability to absorb or release energy** in the form of
a [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon). **An energy gain can
trigger the electron to move (jump) to an outer shell**; this is known
as [atomic
excitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state#Atomic_excitation).
Or the electron can even break free from its associated atom\'s shell;
this is [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization) to form a
positive ion. **When an electron loses energy (thereby causing a photon
to be emitted), then it can move to an inner shell which is not fully
occupied.**

# Dissipative system

A **dissipative system** is a thermodynamically [open
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_system_(systems_theory)) which
is operating out of, and often far from, [thermodynamic
equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_equilibrium) in
an environment with which it
exchanges [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) and [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter).
A [tornado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tornado) may be thought of as
a dissipative system. Dissipative systems stand in contrast
to [conservative
systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservative_system).

A **dissipative structure** is a dissipative system that has a dynamical
regime that is in some sense in a reproducible [steady
state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steady_state). This reproducible
steady state may be reached by natural evolution of the system, by
artifice, or by a combination of these two.

# Conservative System

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics),
a **conservative system** is a [dynamical
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system) which stands in
contrast to a [dissipative
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipative_system). Roughly
speaking, such systems have
no [friction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friction) or other mechanism
to dissipate the dynamics, and thus, their [phase
space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space) does not shrink over
time. Precisely speaking, they are those dynamical systems that have a
null [wandering set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wandering_set): under
time evolution, no portion of the phase space ever \"wanders away\",
never to be returned to or revisited. Alternately, conservative systems
are those to which the Poincaré recurrence theorem applies. An important
special case of conservative systems are the [measure-preserving
dynamical
systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure-preserving_dynamical_system).

# Phase Space

In [dynamical system
theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems_theory),
a **phase space** is
a [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_(mathematics)) in which
all possible states of
a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System) are represented, with
each possible state corresponding to one unique point in the phase
space. For [mechanical
systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics), the phase
space usually consists of all possible values
of [position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_(vector)) and [momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum) variables.
It is the [outer
product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_product) of direct space
and [reciprocal space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_space).
The concept of phase space was developed in the late 19th century
by [Ludwig
Boltzmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Boltzmann), [Henri
Poincaré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henri_Poincar%C3%A9),
and [Josiah Willard
Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josiah_Willard_Gibbs).[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space#cite_note-1)

**Statistical Mechanics:**

In [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics), **statistical
mechanics** is a mathematical framework that applies [statistical
methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistics) and [probability
theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_theory) to large
assemblies of microscopic entities. It does not assume or postulate any
natural laws, but explains the macroscopic behavior of nature from the
behavior of such ensembles.

Statistical mechanics arose out of the development of [classical
thermodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_thermodynamics),
a field for which it was successful in explaining macroscopic physical
properties---such
as [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature), [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure),
and [heat capacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_capacity)---in
terms of microscopic parameters that fluctuate about average values and
are characterized by [probability
distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_distribution).
This established the fields of **statistical
thermodynamics** and [statistical
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics).

# Electromagnet

An **electromagnet** is a type
of [magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) in which the [magnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field) is produced by
an [electric current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current).
Electromagnets usually consist of wire wound into
a [coil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil). A current
through the wire creates a magnetic field which is concentrated in the
hole, denoting the center of the coil. The magnetic field disappears
when the current is turned off. The wire turns are often wound around
a [magnetic core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_core) made from
a [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic) or [ferrimagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrimagnetic) material
such as [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron); the magnetic core
concentrates the [magnetic
flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux) and makes a more
powerful magnet.

The main advantage of an electromagnet over a [permanent
magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permanent_magnet) is that the
magnetic field can be quickly changed by controlling the amount of
electric current in the winding. **However, unlike a permanent magnet
that needs no power, an electromagnet requires a continuous supply of
current to maintain the magnetic field.**

Electromagnets are widely used as components of other electrical
devices, such
as [motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor), [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator), [electromechanical
solenoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solenoid#Applications), [relays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relay), [loudspeakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loudspeaker), [hard
disks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_disk), [MRI
machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging),
scientific instruments, and [magnetic
separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_separation) equipment.
Electromagnets are also employed in industry for picking up and moving
heavy iron objects such as scrap iron and
steel.[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet#cite_note-Merzouki-2)

# Electromagnetic Coil

An **electromagnetic coil** is an
electrical [conductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductivity) such
as a [wire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire) in the shape of
a [coil](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/coil) ([spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral) or [helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helix)).^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-Stauffer-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-Laplante-2)^ Electromagnetic
coils are used in [electrical
engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), in
applications where [electric
currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) interact
with [magnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field), in
devices such as [electric
motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor), [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_generator), [inductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductor), [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet), [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer),
and sensor coils. Either an electric current is passed through the wire
of the coil to generate a magnetic field, or conversely, an
external *time-varying* magnetic field through the interior of the coil
generates
an [EMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromotive_force) ([voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage))
in the conductor.

A current through any conductor creates a circular magnetic field around
the conductor due to [Ampere\'s
law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampere%27s_circuital_law).[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-Arun-3) The
advantage of using the coil shape is that it increases the strength of
the magnetic field produced by a given current. The magnetic fields
generated by the separate turns of wire all pass through the center of
the coil and add
([superpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superposition_principle)) to
produce a strong field
there.[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-Arun-3) The
more turns of wire, the stronger the field produced. Conversely,
a *changing* external magnetic flux induces a voltage in a conductor
such as a wire, due to [Faraday\'s law of
induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday%27s_law_of_induction).^[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-Arun-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-4)^ The
induced voltage can be increased by winding the wire into a coil because
the field lines intersect the circuit multiple
times.[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_coil#cite_note-Arun-3)

The direction of the magnetic field produced by a coil can be determined
by the [right hand grip
rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_hand_grip_rule). If the
fingers of the right hand are wrapped around the magnetic core of a coil
in the direction of [conventional
current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventional_current) through the
wire, the thumb will point in the direction the [magnetic field
lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field_line) pass through
the coil. The end of a magnetic core from which the field lines emerge
is defined to be the North pole.

There are many different types of coils used in electric and electronic
equipment.

# Ampère\'s Circuital Law

In [classical
electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_electromagnetism), **Ampère\'s
circuital law** (not to be confused with [Ampère\'s force
law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amp%C3%A8re%27s_force_law) that [André-Marie
Ampère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andr%C3%A9-Marie_Amp%C3%A8re) discovered
in
1823)[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amp%C3%A8re%27s_circuital_law#cite_note-1) relates
the [integrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_integral) [magnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field) around a closed
loop to the [electric
current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) passing through
the loop. [James Clerk
Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell) (not Ampère)
derived it
using [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_dynamics) in
his 1861 published paper \"[On Physical Lines of
Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:On_Physical_Lines_of_Force.pdf)\"[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amp%C3%A8re%27s_circuital_law#cite_note-2) In
1865 he generalized the equation to apply to time-varying currents by
adding the [displacement
current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_current) term,
resulting in the modern form of the law, sometimes called
the **Ampère--Maxwell
law**,^[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amp%C3%A8re%27s_circuital_law#cite_note-Fleisch-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amp%C3%A8re%27s_circuital_law#cite_note-Garg-4)[\[5\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amp%C3%A8re%27s_circuital_law#cite_note-Katz-5)^ which
is one of [Maxwell\'s
equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_equations) which
form the basis
of [classical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics) [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism).

# Magnetostatics

**Magnetostatics** is the study of [magnetic
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field) in systems where
the [currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_currents) are
steady (not changing with time). It is the magnetic analogue
of [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics), where
the [charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge) are
stationary. The magnetization need not be static; the equations of
magnetostatics can be used to predict fast [magnetic
switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetization_reversal) events
that occur on time scales of nanoseconds or
less.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetostatics#cite_note-Hiebert-1) **Magnetostatics
is even a good approximation when the currents are not static --- as
long as the currents do not alternate rapidly.** Magnetostatics is
widely used in applications of micromagnetics such as models
of [magnetic
storage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_storage) devices as
in [computer memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory).

# Magnetic Moment

In [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism),
the **magnetic moment** is the magnetic strength and orientation of
a [magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) or other object that
produces a [magnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field). Examples of
objects that have magnetic moments include loops of [electric
current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) (such
as [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet)),
permanent magnets, [elementary
particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) (such
as [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron)),
various [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule), and many
astronomical objects (such as
many [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet),
some [moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite), [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star),
etc).

More precisely, the term *magnetic moment* normally refers to a
system\'s **magnetic dipole moment**, the component of the magnetic
moment that can be represented by an equivalent [magnetic
dipole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole): a magnetic north
and south pole separated by a very small distance. The magnetic dipole
component is sufficient for small enough magnets or for large enough
distances. Higher-order terms (such as the [magnetic quadrupole
moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrupole_moment)) may be needed
in addition to the dipole moment for extended objects.

# Magnetic monopole

In [particle physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics),
a **magnetic monopole** is a hypothetical [elementary
particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) that is an
isolated [magnet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) with only one
magnetic pole (a north pole without a south pole or vice
versa).^[\[1\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#cite_note-Hooper-1)[\[2\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#cite_note-2)^ A
magnetic monopole would have a net north or south \"magnetic charge\".
Modern interest in the concept stems from [particle
theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-energy_physics), notably
the [grand
unified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_unified_theory) and [superstring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstring_theory) theories,
which predict their
existence.^[\[3\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#cite_note-3)[\[4\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#cite_note-4)^ The
known elementary particles that have [electric
charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_charge) are electric
monopoles.

Magnetism in [bar
magnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bar_magnet) and [electromagnets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnet) is
not caused by magnetic monopoles, and indeed, there is no known
experimental or observational evidence that magnetic monopoles exist.

Some [condensed
matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter) systems contain
effective (non-isolated) magnetic
monopole [quasi-particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiparticle),[^\[5\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#cite_note-Castelnovo-5) or
contain phenomena that are mathematically analogous to magnetic
monopoles.[^\[6\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_monopole#cite_note-Ray-6)

# Condensed matter physics

**Condensed matter physics** is the field
of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) that deals with the
macroscopic and microscopic physical properties
of [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter), especially
the [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) and [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) [phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_of_matter) which
arise
from [electromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic) forces
between [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom). More generally, the
subject deals with \"condensed\" phases of matter: systems of many
constituents with strong interactions between them. More exotic
condensed phases include
the [superconducting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity) phase
exhibited by certain materials at
low [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature),
the [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnet) and [antiferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antiferromagnet) phases
of [spins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_(physics)) on [crystal
lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice) of atoms, and
the [Bose--Einstein
condensate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose%E2%80%93Einstein_condensate) found
in [ultracold
atomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultracold_atom) systems. Condensed
matter physicists seek to understand the behavior of these phases by
experiments to measure various material properties, and by applying
the [physical
laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law) of [quantum
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics), [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), [statistical
mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_mechanics), and
other [theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics) to
develop mathematical models.

The diversity of systems and phenomena available for study makes
condensed matter physics the most active field of contemporary physics:
one third of all American physicists self-identify as condensed matter
physicists,[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics#cite_note-1) and
the Division of Condensed Matter Physics is the largest division at
the [American Physical
Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Physical_Society).[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics#cite_note-aps-history-2) The
field overlaps
with [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), [materials
science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science), [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) and [nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanotechnology),
and relates closely to [atomic
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_physics) and [biophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysics).
The [theoretical
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physics) of condensed
matter shares important concepts and methods with that of [particle
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle_physics) and [nuclear
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics).[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics#cite_note-marvincohen2008-3)

# Atomic physics

**Atomic physics** is the field
of [physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics) that
studies [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) as an isolated
system of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) and
an [atomic nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus).
Atomic physics typically refers to the study of atomic structure and the
interaction between
atoms.[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_physics#cite_note-1) It
is primarily concerned with [the way in which electrons are arranged
around the
nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration) and the
processes by which these arrangements change. This
comprises [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion), neutral atoms and,
unless otherwise stated, it can be assumed that the term *atom* includes
ions.

The term *atomic physics* can be associated with [nuclear
power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power) and [nuclear
weapons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon), due to
the [synonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym) use
of *atomic* and *nuclear* in [standard
English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_English). Physicists
distinguish between atomic physics---which deals with the atom as a
system consisting of a nucleus and electrons---and [nuclear
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics), which
studies [nuclear
reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reaction) and special
properties of [atomic
nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus).

Atomic physics primarily considers atoms in isolation form. Atomic
models will consist of a single nucleus that may be surrounded by one or
more bound electrons. It is not concerned with the formation
of [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) (although much of
the physics is identical), nor does it examine atoms in a [solid
state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid-state_physics) as [condensed
matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter). It is concerned
with processes such
as [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization) and [excitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state) by
photons or collisions with atomic particles.

While modelling atoms in isolation may not seem realistic, if one
considers atoms in
a [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas) or [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_(physics)) then
the time-scales for atom-atom interactions are huge in comparison to the
atomic processes that are generally considered. This means that the
individual atoms can be treated as if each were in isolation, as the
vast majority of the time they are. By this consideration atomic physics
provides the underlying theory in [plasma
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_(physics)) and [atmospheric
physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_physics), even though
both deal with very large numbers of atoms.

Electrons form
notional [shells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_shells) around
the nucleus. These are normally in a [ground
state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state) but can be excited by
the absorption of energy from light
([photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon)), magnetic fields, or
interaction with a colliding particle (typically ions or other
electrons).

Electrons that populate a shell are said to be in a [bound
state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_state). The energy necessary
to remove an electron from its shell (taking it to infinity) is called
the [binding energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binding_energy). Any
quantity of energy absorbed by the electron in excess of this amount is
converted to [kinetic
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_energy) according to
the [conservation of
energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_energy). The atom
is said to have undergone the process
of [ionization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization).

If the electron absorbs a quantity of energy less than the binding
energy, it will be transferred to an [excited
state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state). After a certain
time, the electron in an excited state will \"jump\" (undergo a
transition) to a lower state. In a neutral atom, the system will emit a
photon of the difference in energy, since energy is conserved.

If an inner electron has absorbed more than the binding energy (so that
the atom ionizes), then a more outer electron may undergo a transition
to fill the inner orbital. In this case, a visible photon or
a [characteristic
x-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_x-ray) is emitted,
or a phenomenon known as the [Auger
effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auger_effect) may take place,
where the released energy is transferred to another bound electron,
causing it to go into the continuum. The Auger effect allows one to
multiply ionize an atom with a single photon.

There are rather strict [selection
rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_rule) as to the
electronic configurations that can be reached by excitation by light ---
however there are no such rules for excitation by collision processes.

**[Spectral Line:]{.underline}**

A **spectral line** is a dark or bright line in an otherwise uniform
and [continuous
spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_spectrum), resulting
from [emission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emission_(electromagnetic_radiation)) or [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_(electromagnetic_radiation)) of [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) in
a narrow frequency range, compared with the nearby frequencies. Spectral
lines are often used to
identify [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom) and [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule).
These \"fingerprints\" can be compared to the previously collected ones
of
atoms[^\[1\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line#cite_note-1) and
molecules,[^\[2\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line#cite_note-RothmanGordon2013-2) and
are thus used to identify the atomic and molecular components
of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet),
which would otherwise be impossible.

Spectral lines are the result of interaction between a [quantum
system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_mechanics) (usually [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atom),
but
sometimes [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) or [atomic
nuclei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus)) and a
single [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon). When a photon has
about the right amount
of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_energy) (which is
connected to its
frequency)[^\[3\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line#cite_note-3) to
allow a change in the energy state of the system (in the case of an atom
this is usually
an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) changing [orbitals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_configuration)),
the photon is absorbed. **Then the energy will be spontaneously
re-emitted, either as one photon at the same frequency as the original
one or in a cascade, where the sum of the energies of the photons
emitted will be equal to the energy of the one absorbed (assuming the
system returns to its original state).**

A spectral line may be observed either as an **emission line** or
an **absorption line**. Which type of line is observed depends on the
type of material and its temperature relative to another emission
source. An absorption line is produced when photons from a hot, broad
spectrum source pass through a cooler material. The intensity of light,
over a narrow frequency range, is reduced due to absorption by the
material and re-emission in random directions. By contrast, a bright
emission line is produced when photons from a hot material are detected,
perhaps in the presence of a broad spectrum from a cooler source. The
intensity of light, over a narrow frequency range, is increased due to
emission by the hot material.

Spectral lines are highly atom-specific, and can be used to identify the
chemical composition of any medium. Several elements,
including [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium), [thallium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thallium),
and caesium, were discovered by spectroscopic means. Spectral lines also
depend on
the [temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temperature) and [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_density) of
the material, so they are widely used to determine the physical
conditions of [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and other
celestial bodies that cannot be analyzed by other means.

**Depending on the material and its physical conditions, the energy of
the involved photons can vary widely,** with the spectral lines observed
across the [electromagnetic
spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum),
from [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave) to [gamma
rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray).

**Electromagnetic Spectrum:**

The **electromagnetic spectrum** is the range
of [frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency) (the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum))
of [electromagnetic
radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_radiation) and
their
respective [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) and [photon
energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_energy).

The **electromagnetic spectrum** covers electromagnetic **waves with
frequencies ranging from below
one [hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz_(unit)) to above
10^25^ hertz, corresponding
to [wavelengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) from
thousands of [kilometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilometer) down
to a fraction of the size of an [atomic
nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus). This frequency
range is divided into separate bands, and the [electromagnetic
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave) within each
frequency band are called by different names; beginning at the low
frequency (long wavelength) end of the spectrum these are:** [radio
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave), [microwaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave), [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared), [visible
light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_light), [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet), [X-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray),
and [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray) at the
high-frequency (short wavelength) end. The electromagnetic waves in each
of these bands have different characteristics, such as how they are
produced, how they interact with matter, and their practical
applications. There is no known limit for long and short wavelengths.
**Extreme ultraviolet, soft X-rays, hard X-rays and gamma rays are
classified as [ionizing
radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionizing_radiation) because
their photons have enough energy
to [ionize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionization) atoms, causing
chemical reactions.** Exposure to ionizing radiation can be a health
hazard, causing [radiation
sickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_sickness), [DNA
damage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_damage) and [cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer).
Radiation of visible light and longer wavelengths are classified as
nonionizing radiation because they have insufficient energy to cause
these effects.

**Throughout most of the electromagnetic
spectrum, [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy) can
be used to separate waves of different frequencies, producing a spectrum
of the constituent frequencies. Spectroscopy is used to study the
interactions of electromagnetic waves with
matter.[^\[4\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum#cite_note-em-spectrum-4)**

Humans have always been aware of [visible
light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_light) and [radiant
heat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiant_heat) but for most of
history it was not known that these phenomena were connected or were
representatives of a more extensive principle. The [ancient
Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) recognized that
light traveled in straight lines and studied some of its properties,
including [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_(physics)) and [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction).
Light was intensively studied from the beginning of the 17th century
leading to the invention of important instruments like
the [telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope) and [microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope). [Isaac
Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_Newton) was the first to use
the term *spectrum* for the range of colours that white light could be
split into with a [prism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_(optics)).
Starting in 1666, Newton showed that these colours were intrinsic to
light and could be recombined into white light. A debate arose over
whether light had a wave nature or a particle nature with [René
Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren%C3%A9_Descartes), [Robert
Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke) and [Christiaan
Huygens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christiaan_Huygens) favouring a
wave description and Newton favoring a particle description. Huygens in
particular had a well developed theory from which he was able to derive
the laws of reflection and refraction. Around 1801, [Thomas
Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Young_(scientist)) measured
the [wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavelength) of a light
beam with his [two-slit
experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young%27s_interference_experiment) thus
conclusively demonstrating that light was a wave.

In 1800, [William
Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Herschel) discovered [infrared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared) radiation.[^\[5\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum#cite_note-5) He
was studying the temperature of different colours by moving a
thermometer through light split by a prism. He noticed that the highest
temperature was beyond red. He theorized that this temperature change
was due to \"calorific rays\", a type of light ray that could not be
seen. The next year, [Johann
Ritter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Ritter), working at the
other end of the spectrum, noticed what he called \"chemical rays\"
(invisible light rays that induced certain chemical reactions). These
behaved similarly to visible violet light rays, but were beyond them in
the
spectrum.[^\[6\]^](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_spectrum#cite_note-6) They
were later
renamed [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet) radiation.

The study
of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism) began
in 1820 when [Hans Christian
Ørsted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Christian_%C3%98rsted) discovered
that [electric
currents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_current) produce [magnetic
fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_field) ([Oersted\'s
law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oersted%27s_law)). Light was first
linked to electromagnetism in 1845, when [Michael
Faraday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Faraday) noticed that
the [polarization of
light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarization_of_light) traveling
through a transparent material responded to a magnetic field
(see [Faraday effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_effect)).
During the 1860s, [James Clerk
Maxwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Clerk_Maxwell) developed
four partial [differential
equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equation) ([Maxwell\'s
equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell%27s_equations)) for
the [electromagnetic
field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field). **Two of
these equations predicted the possibility and behavior of waves in the
field. Analyzing the speed of these theoretical waves, Maxwell realized
that they must travel at a speed that was about the known [speed of
light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light). This startling
coincidence in value led Maxwell to make the inference that light itself
is a type of electromagnetic wave.** **Maxwell\'s equations predicted an
infinite range of frequencies of [electromagnetic
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_waves), all
traveling at the speed of light.** This was the first indication of the
existence of the entire
electromagnetic [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum).

**Maxwell\'s predicted waves included waves at very low frequencies
compared to infrared, which in theory might be created by oscillating
charges in an ordinary [electrical
circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_Circuit) of a certain
type. Attempting to prove Maxwell\'s equations and detect such low
frequency electromagnetic radiation, in 1886, the physicist [Heinrich
Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Hertz) built an apparatus
to generate and detect what are now called [radio
waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_wave). Hertz found the waves
and was able to infer (by measuring their wavelength and multiplying it
by their frequency) that they traveled at the speed of light.** Hertz
also demonstrated that the new radiation could be both reflected and
refracted by various [dielectric
media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric), in the same manner as
light. For example, Hertz was able to focus the waves using a lens made
of tree [resin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitch_(resin)). In a later
experiment, Hertz similarly produced and measured the properties
of [microwaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave). These new
types of waves paved the way for inventions such as the [wireless
telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wireless_telegraphy) and
the [radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio).

In 1895, [Wilhelm
Röntgen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_R%C3%B6ntgen) noticed a
new type of radiation emitted during an experiment with an [evacuated
tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube) subjected to a [high
voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_voltage). **He called this
radiation "[x-rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray)" and found that
they were able to travel through parts of the human body but were
reflected or stopped by denser matter such as bones. Before long, many
uses were found for
this [radiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiography).**

The last portion of the electromagnetic spectrum was filled in with the
discovery of [gamma rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_ray). In
1900, [Paul Villard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Villard) was
studying the radioactive emissions
of [radium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radium) when he identified a
new type of radiation that he at first thought consisted of particles
similar to
known [alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpha_particle) and [beta
particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_particle), **but with the
power of being far more penetrating than either.** However, in 1910,
**British physicist [William Henry
Bragg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Bragg) demonstrated
that gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation, not particles, and in
1914, [Ernest
Rutherford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Rutherford) (who had
named them gamma rays in 1903 when he realized that they were
fundamentally different from charged alpha and beta particles)**
and [Edward
Andrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Andrade) **measured their
wavelengths, and found that gamma rays were similar to X-rays, but with
shorter wavelengths.**

The wave-particle debate was rekindled in 1901 when [Max
Planck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Planck) discovered that light
is only absorbed in discrete
\"[quanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum)\", now
called [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon), implying that
light has a particle nature. This idea was made explicit by [Albert
Einstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) in 1905, but
never accepted by Planck and many other contemporaries. **The modern
position of science is that electromagnetic radiation has both a wave
and a particle nature, the** [wave-particle
duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave-particle_duality). The
contradictions arising from this position are still being debated by
scientists and philosophers.

### Determinant definition

Although we have already seen lessons on how to obtain determinants such
as the [determinant of a 2x2
matrix](https://www.studypug.com/linear-algebra/the-determinant-of-a-2-x-2-matrix) and
the [determinant of a 3x3
matrix](https://www.studypug.com/linear-algebra/the-determinant-of-a-3-x-3-matrix-general-and-shortcut-method),
we have not taken a moment to define what a matrix determinant is on
itself. Therefore, this lesson will be dedicated to that, to learn the
significance of matrix determinants and their properties, rules and even
some of its applications.

A determinant is a scaling factor for the array of numbers found in a
matrix which can give us information about these values as components of
a vector system or a linear equation system. The first thing to remember
when working through these linear algebra operations is that we can only
calculate determinants of squared matrices, which are those with the
same amount of rows and columns (thus the \"squared\" name, since their
width is the same as their length in terms of quantities of rows and
columns), and so, a determinant is that informative factor that comes
from a system of equations containing the same amount of unknowns as
vectors, which then can be solved to find the value of each unknown.
Thanks to the determinant we can also calculate a matrix inverse.

When the determinant of a square matrix is zero, we call this a singular
matrix. Recalling from lessons on [the inverse of a 2 x 2
matrix](https://www.studypug.com/linear-algebra/the-inverse-of-a-2-x-2-matrix) and [the
inverse of 3 x 3 matrices with matrix row
operations](https://www.studypug.com/linear-algebra/the-inverse-of-3-x-3-matrices-with-matrix-row-operations),
we know that the operation of inverting a matrix happens to be the
reciprocal of the matrix and thus, you can only obtain the inverse a
matrix if the result multiplied by the original matrix produces the
identity matrix.

How is this important when talking about singular matrices? Well, in
order to obtain the inverse of a matrix the general process requires you
to obtain the determinant of the original matrix, swapping the positions
of the values in the original and dividing this new matrix by the
determinant gotten. Therefore, for a singular matrix this would mean to
divide by zero, which cannot be done, and so, singular matrices are not
invertible. Whenever you obtain a zero value as the result of a
determinant, remember this matrix cannot be inverted. This note can
simplify the process of certain problems you may need to solve in the
future.

A [linear
transformation](https://mathinsight.org/linear_transformation_definition_euclidean) T:Rn→RmT:Rn→Rm [(confused?)](https://mathinsight.org/function_notation) is
a mapping from nn-dimensional space to mm-dimensional space. Such a
linear transformation can be [associated with
an m×nm×n matrix](https://mathinsight.org/matrices_linear_transformations).

If we restrict ourselves to mappings within the same space, such
as T:Rn→RnT:Rn→Rn, then TT is associated with a square n×nn×n matrix.
One can calculate
the [determinant](https://mathinsight.org/matrices_determinants_multivariable_calculus) of
such a square matrix, and such determinants are [related to area or
volume](https://mathinsight.org/relationship_determinants_area_volume).
It turns out that the determinant of a matrix tells us important
geometrical properties of its associated linear transformation. We\'ll
outline this relationship
for [one-dimensional](https://mathinsight.org/determinant_linear_transformation#lintrans1D), [two-dimensional](https://mathinsight.org/determinant_linear_transformation#lintrans2D),
and [three-dimensionional](https://mathinsight.org/determinant_linear_transformation#lintrans3D) linear
transformations.

#### **One-dimensional linear transformations**

A one-dimensional linear transformation is a function T(x)=axT(x)=ax for
some [scalar](https://mathinsight.org/definition/scalar) aa. To view the
one-dimensional case in the same way we view higher dimensional linear
transformations, we can view aa as a 1×11×1 matrix. The determinant of
the 1×11×1 matrix is just the number aa itself. Although this case is
very simple, we can gather some intuition about linear maps by first
looking at this case.

An example one-dimensional linear transformation is the
function T(x)=3xT(x)=3x. We could visualize this function by its graph,
which is a line through the origin with slope 3. However, instead,
let\'s view it as a mapping from the real line RR back onto the real
line RR. In this case, we think of the function as x′=T(x)x′=T(x), which
maps the number xx on the xx-axis to the new number T(x)T(x) on
an x′x′-axis. TT takes the number 1 and maps it to 3. TT maps 0 to 0 and
-1/2 to -3/2. We also use the language that 3 is the image of 1 under
the mapping TT.

We can summarize this mapping by looking how TT maps an interval of
numbers. For example, TT maps the interval \[0,1\]\[0,1\] to the
interval \[0,3\]\[0,3\], as illustrated by the following figure. We
colored the interval \[0,1\]\[0,1\] and its image \[0,3\]\[0,3\] with a
green to red gradient that illustrates how each point in the interval is
mapped by TT. A point of a given color in \[0,1\]\[0,1\] is mapped to a
point of the same color in the image \[0,3\]\[0,3\].

![Stretching by the linear transformation
T(x)=3x](media/image29.png){width="5.876388888888889in" height="1.0in"}

The fact that the determinant of the matrix associated with T is 3 means
that T stretches objects so that their length is increased by a factor
of 3. Since the determinant was positive, T preserves the orientation of
objects: in both the interval \[0,1\] and its image \[0,3\], red points
are to the right of green points.

Another example is the linear transformation T(x)=−12x. As shown in the
below figure, T maps the interval \[0,1\] onto the interval \[−1/2,0\].
The determinant of the matrix associated with TT is −1/2. Since the
magnitude of this determinant is 1/2, T shrinks objects to be half their
original length. The negative determinant indicates that T reverses the
orientation of objects: as indicated by the colors, the right of the
interval \[0,1\] is mapped onto the left of the interval \[−1/2,0\].

![Compressing and reflecting by the linear transformation
T(x)=-x/2](media/image30.png){width="5.876388888888889in"
height="1.0097222222222222in"}

In general, the linear transformation T(x)=ax stretches objects to
change their length by a factor of \|a\|. If a is positive, T preserves
the orientation; if a is negative, T reverses orientation. We will
obtain similar conclusions for higher-dimensional linear transformations
in terms of the determinant of the associated matrix.

#### **The determinant of a matrix inverse**

If one applies the linear transformation T(x)=Ax to an object, then
applying the linear transformation T^−1^(x)=A^−1^x should map everything
back to the original object. Since T expands by the factor \|detA\|,
then T^−1^ must do the reverse, expanding by the factor 1/\|det(A). The
linear transformation T^−1^ must also reverse orientation just
when T reverses orientation. In other words, the determinant
of A^‑1^ must be related to the determinant of A by:

det(A^-1^) = 1/det(A)

#### **The effect of multiplying matrices:**

The geometric interpretation allows us to quickly infer the determinant
of a product A.B for n×n matrices A and B. If we apply the linear
transformation S(x)=ABx to an object, it\'s the same thing as first
applying the linear transformation T'(x)=Bx and then applying the linear
transformation T(x)=Ax. (This follows from the fact that matrix
multiplication is associative.)

Since T expands by a factor of \|det(A)\|, and T' expands by a
factor \|det(B)\|, applying both transformations in succession must
expand by a factor \|det(A)\|\|det(B)\|. Moreover, the combination of
the two transformations can reverse orientation only if just one
of T or T' orientation. We can summarize these observations with an
equation for det (AB) that reflects the properties of the combined
linear transformation S:

det (AB)=det(A)det(B)

#### **The effect of scaling a matrix**

Since a linear transformation [can always be
written](https://mathinsight.org/matrices_linear_transformations) as T(x)=
Ax for some matrix A, applying a linear transformation to a vector x is
the same thing as multiplying by a matrix. For square matrices A, the
absolute value of the determinant captures how applying TT [expands or
compresses
objects](https://mathinsight.org/determinant_linear_transformation). The
nature of the expansion or compression depends on the underlying
dimension. One-dimensional linear transformations expand length by a
factor \|det(A)\|, two-dimensional linear transformations expand area by
a factor \|det(A)\|, and three-dimensional linear transformations expand
volume by a factor \|det(A)\|.

How would the expansion of the linear transformation be affected if we
scaled A? For example, if we doubled every entry of A, forming the
matrix B=2A, how would the expansion of the linear
transformation T'=Bx compare with that of the original T=Ax? In one
dimension, the effect of doubling every vector would simply double the
expansion of length by T. But, in two dimensions, doubling both
components of every vector would lead to increasing the area expansion
of T by a factor of 2^2^=4, compared to the area expansion of T.
Similarly, doubling every component of the matrix associated with a
three-dimensional T' would expand volume 2^3^=8 times more than the
original T. Doubling every dimension of a three-dimensional object must
lead to the eight-fold increase in volume.

Since this expansion is captured by \|det(A)\|, we conclude
that \|det(2A)\| =2\|det(A)\| in one-dimension, 
\|det(2A)\|=2^2^\|det(A)\| in two-dimensions,
and \|det(2A)\|=2^3^\|det(A)\| in three-dimensions. It turns out this
works in higher dimensions (where a linear transformation expands
higher-dimensional volume), and \|det(2A) \|=2^n^\|det(A)\| in for
an n×n matrix A.

There was, of course, nothing special about the number 2. By the same
geometric argument, if we multiply every component of the matrix A by a
positive number c, we will change the resulting expansion of T by the
factor c in each of it dimensions. For an n-dimensional linear
transformation, this multiplication will change its overall expansion by
the factor c^n^. Hence, we can conclude that for an n×n matrix A.
\|det(cA)\|=c^n^\|det(A)\|.

What about negative numbers and the sign of the determinant? The sign of
the determinant determines whether a linear transformation [preserves or
reverses
orientation](https://mathinsight.org/determinant_linear_transformation).
In one dimension, multiplying the one component of the matrix by a
negative number would correspond to reflecting in that one dimension.
Therefore, multiply by a negative number would change the size of the
determinant. We can conclude that for one dimension, det(cA)=c det(A)
for any number c. (This result is painfully obvious since the
determinant is just equal to the one number of A, but we discuss the
one-dimensional case just to give intuition on higher dimensions.)

In two dimensions, the situation is different. If we multiplied every
component of A by a negative number, the change would correspond to
flipping an object once across the x-axis and then once across
the y-axis. Each of these reflections would change the orientation, but
the two reflections cancel each other out. We cannot change the
influence of a two-dimensional linear transformation T on orientation
through multiplying all components of its matrix A by a negative number.
Combining this result with the expansion due to the magnitude of c, we
conclude that det(cA)=c^2^det(A) in two dimensions. This equation
summarizes how multiplication by a number cannot change the determinant
sign in two dimensions.

In three-dimensions, multiplying the matrix A by a negative number does
change the influence of T on orientation. The net effect of a reflection
across each of the three coordinate axes would still be a change of
orientation. Since any odd number of reflections would change
orientation, we see that multiplying the determinant by c^n^ will
capture the effect on orientation in n-dimensions. We conclude that for
an n×n matrix A and a real number c, det(cA) = c^n^det(A).

**Pivot Position:**\
A pivot position in a matrix, A, is a position in the matrix that
corresponds to a row--leading 1 in the [reduced row echelon
form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_row_echelon_form) of A.
Since the reduced row echelon form of A is unique, the pivot positions
are uniquely determined and do not depend on whether or not row
interchanges are performed in the reduction process. Also, the pivot of
a row must appear to the right of the pivot in the above row in [row
echelon form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_echelon_form).

**Positive Definiteness:**

A matrix is positive definite if it's symmetric and all its eigenvalues
are positive. The thing is, **there are a lot of other equivalent ways
to define a positive definite matrix. One equivalent definition can be
derived using the fact that for a symmetric matrix the signs of the
pivots are the signs of the eigenvalues. So, for example, if a 4 × 4
matrix has three positive pivots and one negative pivot, it will have
three positive eigenvalues and one negative eigenvalue.** This is proven
in section 6.4 of the textbook. We can apply this fact to positive
definite matrices to derive the next equivalent definition. **A matrix
is positive definite if it's symmetric and all its pivots are
positive.** **Pivots are, in general, way easier to calculate than
eigenvalues. Just perform elimination and examine the diagonal terms.**
