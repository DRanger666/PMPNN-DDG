Abstract:

Accurate and tractable approximation of single-point mutation-induced
change in protein thermodynamic stability (denoted by ∆∆G/DDG) is
critical for understanding the genotype-phenotype relationships. Several
computational methods with varying degrees of accuracies and resource
requirements have been proposed for this problem. However, the
inherently complex relationship between mutations and their structural
stability, limited training data, and experimental noise in DDG
calculation makes it a significantly challenging task. Consequently, the
computational predictors proposed throughout the past decade have
attempted incremental prediction performance improvements by proposing
novel features, combining existing features, task-adapted neural network
architectures, loss functions, data augmentation techniques, and
pre-training procedures, for addressing gaps in the previous methods. In
this work, we propose PMPNN-DDG, a Random Forest-based DDG prediction
model, trained on a novel set of interpretable features extracted from
the recently proposed message-passing neural network-based fixed
backbone protein design model, ProteinMPNN. Empirical evaluation shows
that PMPNN-DDG outperforms existing state-of-the-art DDG prediction
methods on *S_669* independent test-set with respect to relevant
performance measures. Similar favorable performance trends were observed
on *Ssym* independent test-set, thus further corroborating the efficacy
of our proposed pipeline. PMPNN-DDG is publicly available at.

1\. Introduction

Protein structural stability is a critical factor specifying the
function and regulation of these essential biomolecules \[1\].
Consequently, genetic mutations modifying the amino acid sequence of a
protein often result in dysfunction arising from thermodynamic stability
perturbation \[2\] \[3\]. However, experimental quantification of this
perturbation by measuring the folding-unfolding Gibbs free energy change
poses severe time and resource constraints, especially for
high-throughput mutation impact characterization. For addressing these
issues, accurate and fast computational prediction pipelines are
urgently required.

Throughout the past decade, several computational pipelines have been
proposed for predicting single point mutation stability impact \[4\].
Essential components of majority of these pipelines are fast lightweight
machine learning (ML) models that learn a mapping from mutations to
corresponding DDG values. A recent comparison of the performances of
these pipelines \[4\] have identified convolutional neural network (CNN)
based ACDC-NN \[5\], Random Forest (RF) based PremPS \[6\], 3D-CNN and
atom-level voxelization-based ThermoNet \[7\], untrained linear feature
combination-based DDGun3D \[8\], and mutant modeling followed by
wildtype and mutant energy difference estimation-based Rosetta \[9\], as
some of the top performing DDG prediction methods.

The training datasets generally contain more instances of destabilizing
mutations (DDG\>0) compared to stabilizing ones (DDG\<0). This results
in significant prediction bias towards destabilizing variants. For
mitigating this issue, ACDC-NN, PremPS, Thermonet, and few other
recently proposed predictors have included reverse instances for each of
the mutations in the training set, thus increasing the size of the
training sets and removing skewness of the experimental label
distribution. This augmentation has been shown to be effective in
enforcing the highly desirable antisymmetric property \[10\] of these
predictors while improving overall prediction performance \[4\].
However, we argue that since the structural features of PremPS are
extracted based on residue-level spatial information \[6\], values
across those dimensions would be very similar for corresponding forward
and reverse mutant pairs. This will result in potentially low
information content of those structural features when
reverse-mutant-based augmentation is applied. This could be one of the
issues behind relatively low importance ranking of these features
compared to the sequence-based evolutionary features in PremPS \[6\].

On the other hand, ACDC-NN \[5\], "marginally" outperforms its solely
sequence-based counterpart ACDC-NN-Seq \[11\]. ACDC-NN incorporates
structural information through position-specific-scoring-matrix (PSSM)
row encoding based on the local spatial neighborhood of the mutated
residue. We argue that this input representation would be highly similar
between members of wildtype structure and mutant model pairs, which are
passed through a shared Siamese NN \[5\]. Although ACDC-NN is enforced
to learn the antisymmetric relationship between members of these pairs
through a custom loss function \[5\], a more informative input embedding
has the potential to improve overall prediction performance.
Additionally, ACDC-NN highlights the importance of transfer learning
while training ML-based DDG predictors due to limited experimentally
labelled instances, and attempts to address this issue by pre-training
on predictions from the untrained predictor DDGun3D \[8\].

In a similar line of work, Chu et al. \[12\] utilized embeddings
extracted from BERT-style \[13\] masked protein language model ESM-1b
\[14\] for training graph-neural network (GNN) models on deep mutational
scanning (DMS) datasets. Interestingly, a simple fully connected NN
trained solely on embeddings corresponding to the mutated position was
shown to significantly outperform the structural-geometry aware GNN
models \[12\]. Based on this observation, we hypothesize that more
innovative methods with underlying biophysical interpretation are
required for extracting informative representations from NN models
trained on large sequence and structure databases.

For addressing these issues, in this work, we introduce PMPNN-DDG, where
the recently proposed message passing encoder decoder-based
autoregressive protein design model, ProteinMPNN (PMPNN) \[15\], is
utilized for extracting a minimal set of interpretable features with
significantly non-overlapping information content. These features,
inherently capable of capturing the differences between members of
forward-reverse pairs, can ideally quantify the structural frustration
induced by single point mutations. Subsequently, these are combined with
PSSM-derived evolutionary conservation features, before training a RF
regressor model. Finally, we establish through rigorous evaluation that
inclusion of our proposed features results in PMPNN-DDG outperforming
the existing state-of-the-art DDG prediction models on several
well-established benchmark datasets.

2\. Benchmark Datasets

In this study, we have employed one dataset for training the RF model
and evaluated PMPNN-DDG on three independent test sets. These datasets
are:

1.  S_2648, where 2,648 manually verified variants with experimentally
    measured DDG values were collected from \[16\] in \[17\], is used
    for training and validation. The 2,648 mutations in this dataset
    spans across 131 distinct proteins. We excluded one protein and its
    corresponding mutations as the respective PDB record is obsolete.

2.  S_669, presented recently in \[4\] for unbiased ranking of the
    existing DDG prediction methods, where 669 variants belonging to
    proteins having no more than 25% sequence similarity with any S_2648
    protein was compiled after filtering from the recently presented
    database ThermoMutDB \[18\]. S_669 contains 669 mutations from 94
    distinct proteins.

3.  Ssym, containing 684 variants with experimental crystal structures,
    where half of them are forward mutations, and rest of them are
    corresponding reverse mutations, was presented in \[19\].

4.  S_921, presented in \[6\], containing 921 mutations from 54 proteins
    was used as the third independent test set for additional
    comparison.

It is to be noted that the three independent test sets were kept
separate throughout the model training and hyperparameter tuning stages,
to ensure valid comparison with the top performing methods from the
existing literature.

3\. Description of MPNN-Extracted Features

As mentioned in the introduction section, a novel contribution of this
work is the PMPNN-based feature extraction method, which encodes each
single point mutation using a length-nine real vector. These nine
features will be denoted by A, B, C, D, E-1~,~ E-2, E-3~,~ E~­~-4~,~ and
E-5 throughout the rest of this manuscript (the last five features can
be grouped together as these are generated by the same function, thus E
will be used to denote these five numbers as a set)~.~ Before each of
the five feature generation functions are detailed in individual
subsections, our general procedure to identify most attended neighbors,
mask the identified neighbors one by one, extract corresponding
embedding vectors, corresponding message vectors, and corresponding
probability distributions from PMPNN for quantifying the shift in
structural stability induced by wildtype-to-mutant residue-identity
change, is explained in the following subsection (the phrases "most
attended neighbors", "mask neighbors", "embedding vectors", and "message
vectors" are described as well).

3.1. Neighbor Ranking and Neighbor-Vector Extraction Procedure

We hypothesize that, the changes in the center-\>neighbor message
vectors, and the changes in the neighbor embedding vectors due to the
change in embedding of the center node reflect the structural stability
shift due to a particular single point mutation (since this specific
mutation information provided to PMPNN decoder has caused the message
and embedding vectors to change in the first place with an attempt to
alleviate potential structural frustration). Consequently, we establish
a protocol to systematically extract these vectors for both the wildtype
and the mutated sequences, which is the basis of our features B, C, D,
and E. This protocol is illustrated in **Figure 2**.

![](media/image1.png){width="6.444444444444445in"
height="3.9743055555555555in"}

As depicted in **Figure 2**, the first step is to pass the wildtype
protein through PMPNN, where the wildtype backbone is processed through
the encoder layers before wildtype sequence embeddings are integrated at
the decoder layers. At this step, the mutation position is masked in the
sequence input, thus PMPNN attempts to reconstruct that position based
on full backbone geometry and residue identity of all other positions,
which are unmasked. The mutation position will be referred to as the
"center"/ "center node" throughout the rest of this section, since PMNN
treats the input backbone as a graph, where every residue is connected
to 48 spatially least distant residues (residues with lowest pairwise
Cα-Cα distances) through directed edges, and the 48 residues closest to
the mutation position will be referred to as "neighbors"/ "neighbor
nodes". Next, for each of these 48 neighbors, we extract a 128-length
vector that the center node receives from the respective neighbor at the
last decoder layer to update its own embedding ("neighbor-\>center
message vector"). Consequently, each of these vectors can be regarded as
a specific neighbor's contribution to the center node's identity, thus
we utilize the L2-norms of these vectors for ranking the neighbors based
on their interaction strength with mutated position in the wildtype
structure (higher L2-norm would indicate stronger interaction) and
select *K* neighbors with the highest norms (*K* was set to 15 based on
empirical observations). These neighbors, referred to as "Top K
Neighbors" in **Figure 2**, can also be regarded as "the most attended
neighbors" by the center node, since some hypothetical attention
module's coefficients would be used as scaling factors for the message
vectors before summation \[20\], and we are approximating those
coefficients by taking norms of those vectors since PMPNN does not use
any explicit attention module. The positions of these top-K neighbors
are saved at this point and utilized in the next step of our pipeline.
Additionally, feature A is generated from the probability distribution
corresponding to the masked center node extracted during this step of
our pipeline (this point is discussed further in one of the following
subsections, and the corresponding output vector is denoted by *P~C~* in
**Figure 2**).

In the next step, each of the top-K neighbor positions are masked one by
one separately, while the mutation position is unmasked, set to both the
wildtype residue and the mutant residue, which results in two sets of
vectors (embedding vector, center-\>neighbor message vector, and
probability distribution vector) for each neighbor position, one set
corresponding to the wildtype center, and another set corresponding to
the mutant center. The embedding vector, center-\>neighbor message
vector, and probability distribution corresponding to the wildtype
residue for neighbor *j* are denoted by *E~j~­^WT^*~,~ *M~j~^WT^*~,~ and
*P~j~^WT^*~,~ respectively, whereas the vectors in the same order
corresponding to the mutated residue for neighbor *j* are denoted by
*E­~j~^MT^*~,~ *M~j~^MT^*~,~ and *P~j~^MT^*~,~ respectively. In the
described setting, since the differences between the equivalent vectors
for the same neighbor position (i.e., *E~j~­^WT^ vs. E~j~­^MT^, M~j~^WT^
vs. M~j~^MT^~,~ and P~j~^WT^ vs. P~j~^MT^*) are conditioned on the
change in center embedding due to WT-\>MT mutation, we utilize these
differences for characterizing mutation impact on the most attended
neighbors, and encode these vectors through the features B, C, D, and E,
described in the following subsection.

3.2. Neighbor-Vector Encoding Methods

3.2.1. Neighbor Entropy-Change Summation (B)

Feature B, depicting the change in information entropy \[21\] of the
most attended neighbor position probability distributions, can be
expressed through the following equation:

$$\sum_{j = 1}^{K}{(H\left( P_{j}^{WT} \right) - H\left( P_{j}^{MT} \right))}$$

( )

3.2.2. Center-\>Neighbor Message-Norm-Ratio Summation (C)

Feature C, depicting the change in magnitude of the center-\>neighbor
message vectors (which can be regarded as an approximation of
neighbor-\>center attention according to the intuition discussed while
explaining step one of the neighbor-vector extraction procedure) due to
WT-\>MT mutation, is described through the following equation:

$$\sum_{j = 1}^{K}{(\frac{{||M_{j}^{WT}||}_{2}}{{||M_{j}^{MT}||}_{2}})}$$

height="6.2972222222222225in"}( )

3.2.3. Neighbor Embedding-Change-Norm Summation (D)

Feature D, quantifying the change in neighbor embedding vectors induced
by WT-\>MT mutation, can be expressed as:

$$\sum_{j = 1}^{K}{||E_{j}^{WT} - \ E_{j}^{MT}||}_{2}$$

( )

3.2.4. Center-\>Neighbor Message-Change Kernel-PCA Projection Summation
(E)

Feature E, encoding further discriminatory information regarding
mutation structural stability impact from the changes in
center-\>neighbor message vectors, can be expressed through the
following equation:

$$\sum_{j = 1}^{K}{{RBC}_{1 - 5}(M_{j}^{WT} - \ M_{j}^{MT})}$$

( )

It is to be noted that, unlike B, C, and D, where two-body energy
changes are approximated using scalar values, E returns a length-five
vector (the elements of this vector are denoted by E-1, E-2, E-3, E-4,
and E-5 in a following section), since we have projected the
center-\>neighbor message difference vectors onto the basis spanned by
five leading principal components returned by a non-linear radial basis
function (RBF) kernel-based principal component analysis (PCA) \[22\].
This kernel PCA was trained on randomly selected neighbor positions of
mutations from the S_2648 training set for computational tractability.

3.3. Mutation-Position Energy-Change (A)

The probability distribution corresponding to the mutation position
(depicted as P~C~ in **Figure 2**), extracted during the first step of
our pipeline is utilized for generating feature A, approximating
mutation-position one-body energy change, can be expressed as follows:

$$\ln\left( P_{C}\lbrack{WT}_{i}\rbrack \right) - \ln\left( P_{C}\lbrack{MT}_{i}\rbrack \right)$$

3.4. Feature-Label Correlation and Feature-Feature Decorrelation
Analysis

![](media/image3.png){width="5.180555555555555in"
height="4.236111111111111in"}While extracting the features A, B, C, D,
and E from PMPNN one by one, we attempt to get an initial estimation of
how potent these individually are for the task of experimental DDG
prediction, and how they might perform together when combined through a
downstream ML model. For this purpose, we examine the Pearson
Correlation Coefficient (PCC) of each of the features with experimental
DDG labels along with the pairwise feature-feature PCC values on S_2648
training set, which are reported through the correlation heatmap in
**Figure 4**.

As can be seen from **Figure 4,** among A, B, C, and D, all pairs seem
"sufficiently" decorrelated (e.g., the maximum pairwise PCC of 0.52 is
observed between A and B). On the other hand, feature C shows a PCC of
-0.15 with the corresponding experimental DDG values, which is the
minimum feature-label correlation in terms of PCC magnitude.

Consequently, some interesting patterns emerge after PCC values
corresponding to E-1, E-2, E-3, E-4, and E-5 are included in the
heatmap, which provide insight into the information captured by the PCA
components, and their potential contribution to downstream ML model. E-1
shows a PCC of 0.49 with D, indicating that the maximum variation in
center-\>neighbor message difference vectors could be largely explained
by the changes in corresponding neighbor embedding vectors (this is
somewhat intuitive since some amount of neighbor embedding change can be
attributed to last decoder layer center-\>neighbor message change). On
the other hand, E-2, which has the highest PCC magnitude with
experimental labels among the five PCA-based features, is not
"significantly" correlated with any other feature (maximum PCC of 0.17
with D), thus making it highly probable that, incorporation of E-2 will
boost downstream ML model's performance. Similarly, E-4 has a PCC of
0.21 with the labels but has a maximum PCC of 0.20 with other features,
which makes it another great candidate for incorporation into the
combined feature set. While E-5 has low PCC with both the label column
and the other features (this component could probably be removed without
hurting model performance), E-3 has PCC values of 0.55, 0.40, and 0.55
with A, B, and C, respectively, and PCC of 0.26 with the label column
(features A, B, and C could probably account for this component if it
were to be removed from the final feature set). Furthermore, E-1, E-2,
E-3, E-4, and E-5 seem "sufficiently" decorrelated among themselves
(e.g., the maximum pairwise PCC of 0.20 is observed between E-2 and
E-4).

Based on the above discussion, it can be stated that, our proposed
attributes A, B, C, and D contain significant discriminatory information
with inherent biophysical interpretation, while group E increases
information content of the combined set by supplementing the feature
space with dimensions where abstract aspects of mutation structural
impact are captured.

4\. Description of Evolutionary Features (F, G, and H)

Almost all the top performing sequence-based and/or structure-based DDG
prediction tools (e.g., PremPS \[6\], ACDC-NN \[5\], DDGun3D \[8\],
INPS-Seq \[10\], etc.) compared in \[4\], have explicitly incorporated
sequence-level mutation-induced evolutionary conservation disruption
information extracted through PSI-BLAST \[23\], or HMMER3 \[24\], or
PROVEAN \[25\]; either as manually extracted features, or as part of
sequence embedding provided to NN-based feature extraction modules.
These features have usually been ranked as the top contributing features
\[6\], thus indicating that the downstream ML models or NN architectures
are at some level learning to extract structural stability related
signals from mutation-position and/or mutation-neighborhood evolutionary
patterns, and map those signals to DDG labels. Since our PMPNN features
A, B, C, D, and E, do not contain any explicit multiple sequence
alignment (MSA)- derived information, we explore the possibility of
embellishing our final feature space by adding three MSA-derived
evolutionary conservation features.

Our evolutionary features were extracted from position-specific-scoring
matrices (PSSM) characterizing protein-specific homologous sequences
retrieved by running PSI-BLAST program \[23\] on UniRef90 database
\[26\], using default parameters described in \[6\]. For a particular
mutation, these three features are extracted from the row corresponding
to the PDB sequence position at which the mutation has occurred and the
two columns of that row containing the log-odds ratios corresponding to
the occurrences of the wildtype residue and the alternate residue at the
mutation site. These three evolutionary features are referred to as F,
G, and H, throughout the rest of this manuscript.

Finally, we perform pairwise decorrelation analysis on the full
feature-set (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, and H) to get an estimation of how the
evolutionary features (F, G, and H) might embellish the already
informative feature space spanned by our PMPNN extracted features (A, B,
C, D, and E), which is shown in **Figure 5**.

![](media/image4.png){width="5.597222222222222in"
height="4.590277777777778in"}

As can be seen from **Figure 5,** features F, G, and H show maximum PCC
magnitudes of 0.32, 0.15, and 0.39 (E-1) with A, A, and E-1,
respectively, thus indicating that most of the dimensions in our final
feature space (the space spanned by PMPNN extracted features +
evolutionary features) contain significantly non-overlapping information
encoding different aspects of the mutation under consideration. Having
said that, it is to be re-iterated that, E-3 might be removed without
decreasing performance of the downstream ML model since the variation
along E-3 is explained by A, B, and C (some linear combination of these
three features might recapitulate E-3 with substantially high accuracy),
and E-1 might also be removed due to its PCC magnitudes with both D and
F. However, for this work, we choose to retain all five of the
kernel-PCA projection features, and plan to address this issue with an
attempt to improve downstream ML model performance further as part of
future work.

5\. Regression Model

The primary focus of our current work is to put together a workflow that
can extract a small non-redundant set of informative and interpretable
features from PMPNN, combine those with discriminative and
"sufficiently" decorrelated evolutionary features, and establish the
efficacy of the final feature set in predicting experimental DDG values
by comparing with state-of-the-art methods presented in \[4\], rather
than achieving the maximum possible performance on the independent sets.
Therefore, we have not put much effort into optimal model-type selection
and hyperparameter tuning. We have employed Random Forest (RF) \[27\]
regression implemented in the scikit-learn package \[28\] for mapping
the feature vectors to experimental DDG values.

We performed an extremely coarse-grained manual tuning for
"n_estimators" and "max_samples" hyperparameters on some validation
splits composed of all mutations from 25 randomly selected proteins from
S_2648 training set while training on mutations from rest of the S_2648
proteins. We selected "n_estimators" to be 500 and "max_samples" was set
to 0.5, while

default scikit-learn values for all other hyperparameters were retained.
We acknowledge that,

our predictor's performance could improve further if a grid search-based
extensive hyperparameter tuning was performed on more methodically held
out validation partitions, however, those empirical analyses are out of
the scope of our current work.

6\. Performance Measures

We have employed several widely adopted measures \[4\] for evaluating
the performance of our proposed PMPNN-DDG on the independent test sets,
and comparison with existing state-of-the-art DDG prediction models
\[4\]. These are:

-   Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) between model predictions and
    experimentally determined DDG values.

-   Root mean square error (RMSE) between model predictions and
    experimental DDGs.

-   PCC between model predictions for forward and corresponding reverse
    mutations, which has been used as a measure of DDG predictors'
    antisymmetric property \[4\].

7.1. Feature Contributions:

As can be observed from **Figure 6** for the independents test sets
S_669 and Ssym, respectively, adding features A, B, C, D, E, F, G, and H
one by one during training the RF model on S_2648 training instances
does not result in performance decrease between any two consecutive
feature additions, thus indicating that, our final feature set does not
result in excessive dimensionality-induced overfitting. Moreover, up to
the first evolutionary feature F, each dimension addition results in
performance improvement for both independent sets, thus corroborating
our decorrelation analysis-derived hypothesis regarding non-overlapping
information content of the dimensions in the final space. However,
addition of the last two evolutionary features G and H does not seem to
result in further improvement (more pronounced for S_669), which is
again in agreement with pairwise correlation values (G and H have PCC
values of 0.78 and -0.79 with F, respectively). Nevertheless, we retain
features G and H for now as that does not interfere with the primary
focus of this work. It is to be noted that, for each of the results
reported in **Figure 6,** RF was trained ten times on S_2648, and PCC
values derived from test set predictions of those ten models were
averaged.

7.2. Performance on Forward and Reverse Mutations, and Anti-Symmetry of
Predictions:

Next, we investigate the performance of the RF model trained on our full
feature set (A+B+C+D+E+F+G+H) on forward and reverse mutations of the
test sets separately and report the correlation between predictions for
forward and reverse pairs. Reverse mutations for S_2648 instances were
added during training as done in recent DDG prediction methods \[4\] for
incentivizing the RF model to perform well on both stabilizing and
de-stabilizing mutations. PCC of -1 between forward and reverse
predictions on the test sets would indicate that our predictor is
perfectly antisymmetric, which is a highly desirable yet difficult to
incorporate property for DDG predictors, since free energy change
introduced by a forward mutation plus the change induced by its reverse
mutation should be equal to zero \[10\]. Additionally, since majority of
the forward mutations are in the first quadrant and most of the reverse
mutations are in the third quadrant, correlation between predictions and
experimental DDGs calculated on (forward+reverse) mutations might result
in higher correlation compared to correlations on only forward and only
reverse mutations, thus making it important to check only forward, only
reverse, (forward+reverse), and (forward vs. reverse) correlations for
comprehensive evaluation of a DDG predictor. Only forward correlation,
only reverse correlation, (forward+reverse) correlation, (forward vs.
reverse) correlation, only forward root mean square error (RMSE), only
reverse root mean square error, and (forward+reverse) root mean square
error, denoted by r~F~, r~R~, r~F+R~, r~F-R~, rms~F~, rms~R~, and
rms~F+R,~ respectively, are reported in **Table 1**.

As can be seen from **Table 1,** as mentioned in \[6\], and observed for
every DDG antisymmetric DDG predictor in \[4\], r~F+r~ is significantly
higher than both r~F~ and r~R~ on all three independent test sets.
Moreover, PCC and RMSE on forward and reverse mutations are equal, which
indicate that PMNPP-DDG performs equally well for stabilizing and
destabilizing mutations, and r~F-R~ of -0.99 points towards the
impressive antisymmetric property of predicted DDG values.
